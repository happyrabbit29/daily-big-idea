The Average
Is Lying.
A Little.

How a treatment can win in every group and still lose in the overall numbers. The answer is hiding in the weights.

Edition 0098 November 2026Reading time · 8 minutes
The idea in one sentence

Simpson’s paradox occurs when a trend seen within separate groups disappears or reverses after those groups are combined, because the groups contribute different weights to the overall average.

01 / The opening puzzle

Two treatments. One very rude spreadsheet.

A hospital compares two treatments for the same condition. Treatment A has a higher success rate than Treatment B among patients with mild cases. It also has a higher success rate among patients with severe cases. The result seems straightforward: A wins twice.

Then the hospital combines everyone. Suddenly, Treatment B has the higher success rate overall. The spreadsheet appears to have changed its mind. It has not. The groups were mixed in different proportions: one treatment was given mostly to easier cases, the other mostly to harder ones. The overall percentages are weighted averages, and the weights are doing a lot of work.

This is Simpson’s paradox, a statistical reversal in which a relationship visible in separate subgroups changes, vanishes or flips when the data are aggregated. It is not a mathematical contradiction. Every percentage can be correct at the same time. The trap is assuming that an overall average must tell the same story as the groups that make it up.

Averages do not merely summarise the data. They also inherit the composition of the groups being averaged.

Why? Look at who received each treatment. A was used on 100 severe patients and only 10 mild patients. B was used on 10 severe patients and 100 mild patients. Since mild cases are much more likely to succeed whatever the treatment, B gets a huge boost from having mostly mild cases. A carries the heavier burden of severe cases.

The aggregate rate is not the simple average of the two subgroup percentages. It is a weighted average, with each subgroup weighted by how many people in that treatment group belong to it.

Overall success rate =
(successes in all groups) ÷ (all patients in those groups)

That is the whole trick. The two treatment groups have different compositions, so their weighted averages are not comparing like with like.

03 / A little history

The paradox is older than its name.

The phenomenon was discussed before Edward H. Simpson gave it its famous 1951 treatment. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy traces earlier work to statistician Karl Pearson in 1899 and George Udny Yule in 1903. Simpson’s paper, “The Interpretation of Interaction in Contingency Tables,” helped make the reversal widely known under his name.

Simpson was concerned with how relationships in contingency tables should be interpreted. A table that looks simple when pooled can conceal a different pattern across categories. The modern lesson reaches beyond tables: statistical relationships depend on what has been grouped together, what has been separated, and what the groups represent.

One famous application came in 1975, when Peter Bickel, Eugene Hammel and J. William O’Connell examined graduate admissions at the University of California, Berkeley. The university-wide figures for fall 1973 showed an admission rate of about 44% for men and 35% for women. But department-level analysis gave a more complicated picture: women tended to apply to departments with lower admission rates, and the pattern within departments did not match the aggregate headline.

The authors argued that the pooled data, once departmental decision-making and application patterns were considered, showed a small statistically significant bias in favour of women. This did not prove that discrimination was absent throughout the university or in the wider education system. It showed why a university-wide percentage alone could not identify where or how bias operated.

04 / The deeper lesson

Which question are you actually asking?

Simpson’s paradox is often introduced as a warning about confounding, but two separate questions need to be kept apart.

Question A / What happened?

This is descriptive. What were the observed success rates in the full population and in each subgroup? The answer depends on the data and the chosen level of aggregation.

Question B / What caused it?

This is causal. Would the same patients have had different outcomes if they had received the other treatment? Observed percentages alone may not answer that counterfactual question.

A third variable, such as disease severity, can be associated with both treatment assignment and outcome. In that case, it can confound the comparison. But not every Simpson reversal has the same causal structure. Sometimes the subgroup variable is a genuine pre-existing confounder. Sometimes it is a mediator, a selection variable, or a variable that should not be adjusted for. Conditioning on the wrong variable can create a new bias rather than remove one.

That is why “always split the data” is not a safe statistical rule. The right analysis depends on how the data were generated and on the question being asked. A causal diagram, a study design and subject-matter knowledge can matter more than the visual drama of the reversal.

05 / Where it matters

From hospital wards to business dashboards.

Medicine and public health

Hospitals treat populations with different ages, risk profiles and disease severity. A crude comparison of death or recovery rates can reverse after stratifying by a relevant risk factor. Standardised rates and well-designed trials help make comparisons more meaningful.

Hiring and education

Overall acceptance or promotion rates may conceal differences in job family, seniority, location or applicant pool. Disaggregation can reveal where a disparity occurs, but it does not by itself establish the cause or settle whether the selection process is fair.

Investing and performance

A fund manager may outperform in every market regime but underperform overall if most of the manager’s exposure occurred during a difficult regime. Conversely, an apparently strong aggregate record may come from favourable exposure rather than superior decisions.

Technology and product analytics

A new app feature may improve conversion among both new and returning users, yet the overall conversion rate may fall if the mix of visitors shifts heavily toward a lower-converting segment. Segment mix and within-segment performance are different things.

06 / What the paradox does not say

Do not turn a warning into a new superstition.

It does not mean aggregate data are useless. The overall rate may be exactly the quantity a policymaker or business needs. If the question is “What proportion of all patients recovered?”, the pooled rate answers it. The problem begins when that number is used to answer a different question, such as “Which treatment works better for comparable patients?”

It does not mean subgroup data are automatically superior. Small subgroups can be noisy. Splitting data repeatedly can produce unstable percentages, false discoveries and a forest of tiny comparisons. Groups should be chosen for a defensible reason, ideally before examining the results.

It does not prove that the subgroup comparison is causal. The mild and severe groups in our fictional example may still differ in age, other illnesses or access to care. More adjustment is not always better adjustment. A sound causal analysis requires assumptions about which variables precede treatment, which affect the outcome and how selection happened.

Finally, this is a statistical phenomenon, not a universal law that every average hides a reversal. The reversal is possible under particular combinations of subgroup rates and weights. Often, the aggregate and subgroup patterns point in the same direction. The skill is knowing when the composition of the sample could change the conclusion.

A practical rule: Whenever two groups are compared, ask three things: What is the overall rate? What are the relevant subgroup rates? And how much of each subgroup is represented in each group? Then ask what causal story would justify the comparison.
07 / Try it yourself

Three questions before trusting the chart.

  1. What is the hidden mix?
    Pick a headline comparison: school rankings, hospital outcomes, sales conversion or investment returns. Which background variable could make the groups unlike each other?
  2. Can you reverse the result with weights?
    Invent two subgroups where A beats B in both. Give B more observations in the easier subgroup and A more in the harder one. Calculate the pooled rates. Notice how the weights, not arithmetic errors, create the reversal.
  3. Which number answers the real question?
    If you were allocating a budget, choosing a treatment or evaluating a manager, would you care about the overall outcome, the within-group effect, or both? State the decision first. Then decide which statistic is relevant.
