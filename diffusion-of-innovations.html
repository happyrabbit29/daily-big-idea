How an idea
crosses the room.

One colleague tries a new tool. Another copies the workflow. A manager asks for a pilot. Months later, the whole organisation is talking about it. New ideas rarely spread by magic. They move through people, proof, trust and timing.

Diffusion / the social life of an idea

A new AI assistant arrives at an office. One analyst uses it to make a first-pass summary of a long report. A colleague sees the result and tries it. By Friday, three people have built prompts of their own. A team lead asks about a wider pilot. Then the real questions begin: Can it handle sensitive data? Who checks the output? Does it fit the workflow, and is the time saving genuine?

The technology spread through exposure, observation, experimentation and social proof. The first user needed curiosity; the next needed a colleague's example; the manager needed evidence; the organisation needed a process it could trust.

India offers a current example. Aon reported on 1 July 2026 that 43% of surveyed Indian organisations had deployed AI and another 20% were piloting it. Across Aon's APAC sample, 74% were deploying or piloting. These are organisation-level responses from business and HR leaders, not a count of individual workers.

On 3 September 2026, Microsoft's India findings from its Work Trend Index said 32% of Indian AI users in its study qualified as “Frontier Professionals”, using agents in multi-step workflows and redesigning work. The research covered 20,000 AI users in ten markets alongside Microsoft 365 productivity signals. Microsoft reported that 78% of Indian AI users said AI enabled work they could not do a year earlier.

The denominator matters: 32% refers to AI users in the study, not everyone in India's workforce. Microsoft is also an AI vendor, so its self-reported findings are directional rather than a neutral census. For another population, Pew found 49% of U.S. adults had ever used AI chatbots in a February 2026 survey of 5,119 adults. These measures cannot be directly compared.

Why do some people move first, why do others wait, and what makes an idea cross the room? That is the question behind the Diffusion of Innovations.

01 / THE ORIGINFrom cornfields to communication theory

The theory's roots are not in Silicon Valley but rural Iowa. In the 1930s, researchers studied farmers adopting hybrid seed corn. The new seed promised higher yields, yet farmers did not switch at once: they watched trial plots, spoke to neighbours and weighed the risk of changing a familiar practice.

In 1943, Bryce Ryan and Neal Gross published a landmark study of hybrid seed in two Iowa communities. Farmers often first heard about the seed from commercial sources, but neighbours' experience helped persuade them. Diffusion was a social process, not just a sales pitch.

In 1962, sociologist Everett M. Rogers synthesised research from several fields in Diffusion of Innovations. For Rogers, diffusion is an innovation communicated through channels over time among members of a social system: an idea, a route, a timeline and a community.

An innovation does not spread only because it is better. It spreads when people can see, test, explain and trust its value.
The practical intuition of diffusion theory

02 / THE SHAPEWhy adoption often looks like an S

Plot cumulative adoption. At first the line is almost flat: few know the idea and early users tolerate uncertainty. Then it accelerates as people see results, suppliers improve and training appears. Finally, growth slows as remaining users have different needs or barriers.

The classic adoption curve

Conceptual S-curve: cumulative adoption over time, not a measured AI series.

The S-curve describes many diffusion histories; it is not a timetable. A product can stall, split into rival standards, be mandated or disappear before adoption accelerates.

Rogers grouped adopters by relative timing within a social system. The familiar categories are a classification scheme, not five personality types.

Innovators tolerate uncertainty. Early adopters often become opinion leaders. The early majority want practical proof; the late majority may need lower costs, support or social reassurance. Some never adopt.

“Laggard” is easy to misuse. People may adopt late because a product does not solve their problem, is unaffordable, lacks infrastructure or carries unacceptable risks. Timing is not intelligence.

03 / THE FIVE LEVERSWhat makes an idea easier to adopt?

Rogers identified five perceived characteristics that help explain adoption speed. “Perceived” matters: people respond to what they believe an innovation will do, not only to what designers claim.

AdvantageDoes it seem better than the old way?
CompatibilityDoes it fit existing needs and habits?
ComplexityIs it hard to understand or use?
TrialabilityCan people test it before committing?
VisibilityCan others see its results?

Consider an AI research assistant. Advantage is clearer if it saves time on a repetitive first-pass task. Compatibility improves when it fits approved software and review procedures. Complexity falls with templates. Trialability rises when a team can test it on public documents first. Observability improves when colleagues can inspect the result and compare the time taken.

A flashy demonstration creates awareness; a safe, repeatable workflow can turn curiosity into routine use. The innovation has to fit the user's world.

04 / A WORKED EXAMPLEWhy the first pilot is not the rollout

Imagine a company gives ten employees an AI tool. Two experiment; one finds a reliable use for meeting notes. A respected team lead tries it and shows colleagues the before-and-after. Interest grows, but finance asks about cost, IT about data security, and managers about quality checks.

The next step is not simply buying more licences. The company needs training, approved uses, data rules, human review and evidence of improvement. One department may adopt it, another modify it, and a third reject it.

KnowledgeHear that it exists
PersuasionForm an opinion
DecisionAdopt or reject
ImplementationPut it to use
ConfirmationContinue or reverse

Awareness is not adoption; a pilot is not scale; initial use is not proof of lasting value. People can discontinue an innovation or reinvent it to suit local needs.

The Indian figures illustrate why definitions matter: Aon measures organisations deploying or piloting; Microsoft describes a subset of AI users redesigning workflows. These are different populations and cannot be combined into one national adoption rate.

05 / BEYOND TECHNOLOGYThe theory travels

Public health

A vaccination or screening practice spreads through clinician trust, peer experience, access, convenience and visible community outcomes. A technically effective intervention can still diffuse slowly if delivery is difficult.

Clean energy

Rooftop solar spreads faster when households can see neighbours' installations, understand payback, access financing and trust installers. A strong technology can be blocked by weak institutions.

Consumer culture

Short-form video formats, payment apps and fashion trends spread through imitation and social visibility. The idea is not only the product; it is the behaviour people observe around it.

Organisations

New operating procedures spread when respected peers demonstrate benefits, leaders remove obstacles and the change fits incentives. Mandates can force formal use without creating genuine adoption.

06 / THE REALITY CHECKWhat the model can miss

Diffusion theory is not a universal law. The S-curve is descriptive, adopter categories are a classification scheme, and the five attributes vary in importance by context. A consumer app, medical treatment and mandatory accounting standard do not spread alike.

Rogers also identified research biases. Pro-innovation bias assumes every innovation is good and ought to spread. Individual-blame bias treats non-adopters as the problem while overlooking price, access, infrastructure or a poor product. Retrospective memories of adoption dates can also be faulty.

Adoption is not the same as success. An idea can spread because it is useful, fashionable, subsidised, mandated or simply difficult to avoid. A high adoption rate tells you that behaviour changed. It does not, on its own, tell you whether the change improved people's lives.

Use the theory as a diagnostic: who has heard of the idea, who trusts it, who can try it, who benefits, who is excluded, and what happens after implementation? Do not turn it into a rigid timetable or a label for supposedly backward people.

07 / THINK ABOUT ITThree questions to carry with you

  1. Think of a product or habit you adopted only after friends or colleagues tried it. What evidence changed your mind: an advertisement, an expert, or someone whose judgement you trusted?
  2. Choose an innovation that has not spread in your workplace or community. Which of Rogers' five attributes is weakest: advantage, compatibility, simplicity, trialability or visibility?
  3. Find a technology that became widely adopted but produced mixed results. Who benefited, who carried the costs, and what does the adoption curve fail to show?

When someone says “everyone is using it now”, ask who “everyone” includes. Innovations travel through networks of trust, money, institutions, habits and unequal opportunity.

The first person to try an idea is rarely the whole story. Diffusion begins when somebody else sees it working, decides it may work for them, and carries it one step further.
