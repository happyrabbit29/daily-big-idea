The Winner's
Curse.

Why the bid that wins can be the one built on the most optimistic mistake, and why competition is not proof that a price is sensible.

The idea in one sentence

When everyone is estimating the same uncertain value, the winner is often the bidder whose estimate is highest, which can mean the winner is also the one most likely to have overestimated it.

IS INFORMATION
TOO
01 / The opening scene

A jar of coins, and a very expensive victory.

Imagine an auctioneer places a sealed glass jar on a table. It contains coins worth exactly ₹10,000. Nobody is allowed to open it. Ten people inspect the jar, make their own estimates and submit sealed bids.

Some guess ₹8,900. Others guess ₹9,600, ₹10,200 or ₹11,100. Their estimates are not random nonsense; each person has looked carefully, counted what they can see and made a reasonable attempt. Across the room, the guesses might average close to the true value.

But the auction does not reward the average guess. It rewards the highest bid. The person who wins may be the one who looked at the jar and saw ₹11,100 where there were only ₹10,000. They have won the jar and lost ₹1,100 before the auctioneer has even packed up.

This is the winner's curse: in a competitive setting where bidders are estimating a common, uncertain value, the winning bidder can systematically overpay because winning is more likely when their estimate is unusually optimistic.

The dangerous question is not only “What do I think it is worth?” It is also “What does it mean that I was the one who won?”
02 / The mechanism

The highest estimate is not the most reliable estimate.

Suppose the jar's true value is ₹10,000 and each bidder receives a noisy signal about it. One bidder's estimate lands below the truth, another's above it. If the estimates are roughly centred on the true value, the average can be sensible. But the highest estimate is selected precisely because it sits at the top of the range.

That creates a selection problem. Before the auction, your estimate may be unbiased. After you discover that every other bidder was willing to bid less, you should update: perhaps your information is better, but perhaps you were simply the most optimistic person in the room.

The effect is strongest when the underlying value is common, information is imperfect and bidders do not adequately adjust for the fact that winning selects the highest estimate.

The key phrase is conditional on winning. It is not enough to ask whether your estimate was reasonable before the auction. You must ask whether it remains reasonable given that your bid beat everyone else's.

A tiny numerical illustration
True value: ₹10,000
Your estimate: ₹10,800
Highest competing estimate: ₹10,200
Your winning bid: ₹10,300

Your estimate may have looked plausible in isolation. But the fact that you were the most optimistic bidder is itself a clue. Unless you have genuinely superior information, the win should trigger a valuation check, not a victory lap.

03 / Where the idea came from

Oil companies learned to fear the winning envelope.

In the late 1960s and early 1970s, oil companies competed for offshore leases. The prize was the right to explore and potentially produce oil from a block of seabed. The eventual value depended on uncertain geology, recoverable reserves, extraction costs and future oil prices.

Different companies had different geological interpretations, but they were trying to estimate much the same underlying prize: the economic value of the block. A company that was more optimistic about the reserves or the economics could submit the highest bid. Yet that optimism did not make the oil any more plentiful.

In a landmark 1971 paper, petroleum engineers E. C. Capen, R. V. Clapp and W. M. Campbell examined competitive bidding in these high-risk situations and helped bring the winner's curse into the practical language of the oil industry. Their central warning was brutally useful: before celebrating that a lease has been won, ask why the other informed bidders stopped lower.

The lesson travelled well beyond oil. Whenever bidders are trying to estimate a common prize from imperfect information, the winning bid deserves special scrutiny.

04 / From auctions to boardrooms

The same trap wears a more expensive suit.

Oil, mining and natural resources

Reserve estimates, commodity prices and extraction costs are uncertain. A bidder who assumes a larger deposit or lower operating cost than rivals may win the asset at a price that leaves little room for reality.

Telecom spectrum

Firms bid for licences whose value depends on future demand, pricing, network costs and competitive behaviour. A high bid can reflect a genuinely stronger business case, or a forecast that is simply too rosy.

Mergers and acquisitions

Several buyers may estimate a target's value using overlapping assumptions about growth and synergies. The winning bidder may have forecast the largest benefits and paid away the value it hoped to create.

Corporate procurement

Contractors bidding for a complex project may underestimate cost, time or execution risk. The lowest bid wins the work, then discovers that the spreadsheet was more confident than the construction site.

There is an important distinction in acquisitions. Some value is private to the bidder: a buyer may have distribution, technology or cost synergies that another buyer cannot access. That can make a higher bid rational. The winner's curse is most directly relevant to the part of value that is common or uncertain, not every premium paid in a takeover.

05 / What it is not

Winning is not automatically a mistake.

Private-value auctions are different. If you are bidding for a painting because you love it and value owning it more than anyone else does, your value is genuinely personal. Winning does not necessarily mean you have overestimated a value shared by everyone.

Better information can justify the win. If your geological data, customer insight or operating capability is genuinely superior, you may rationally value an asset more highly than competitors. The right response is to test that information advantage, not assume it exists because you want to win.

Rational bidders can account for the curse. Auction theory does not say that every auction must end in overpayment. A bidder who understands the selection effect adjusts their bid to reflect what winning implies. In equilibrium, the bid may be lower than the bidder's raw estimate. This adjustment is often called bid shading.

Competition can reveal real value. A high price may be justified when several informed bidders independently see strong economics. The point is not to distrust every competitive outcome. It is to avoid treating the winning result as independent confirmation of your own assumptions.

06 / The practical test

Before you raise the bid, interrogate the win.

Whether you are bidding for a company, a contract, a licence or an investment opportunity, separate the asset's underlying value from the fact that you are competing to own it.

  1. Which parts of my valuation are common, and which are genuinely unique to me?
    Separate the shared market opportunity from proprietary information, specific synergies or capabilities that competitors cannot replicate.
  2. What would have to be true for every other bidder to be wrong?
    Identify the assumptions behind your optimism. If the answer is merely “they did not see the opportunity”, demand evidence.
  3. Have I adjusted for the fact that I might win?
    Use scenarios, probability-weighted outcomes and a disciplined bid ceiling. Do not let the competitive process quietly replace your valuation model.
  4. What is my walk-away price?
    Set it before the room gets loud. A pre-committed limit is useful precisely because auctions are designed to make stopping feel like losing.

The best bidder is not necessarily the person who wants the asset most. It may be the person who understands what the asset is worth, what they uniquely bring to it, and when the price has crossed the line.

Further reading / source notes

Follow the trail.

The winner's curse is most clearly analysed in common-value auctions, where participants receive imperfect signals about an underlying value shared across bidders. The concept is associated with both field observations and experimental work.

  1. Richard H. Thaler (1988), “Anomalies: The Winner's Curse”, Journal of Economic Perspectives, 2(1), 191–202. A behavioural-economics introduction using the classic jar-of-coins example.
  2. E. C. Capen, R. V. Clapp and W. M. Campbell (1971), “Competitive Bidding in High-Risk Situations”, Journal of Petroleum Technology, 23(6), 641–653. The influential oil-lease analysis that brought the problem into petroleum bidding practice.
  3. Paul R. Milgrom and Robert J. Weber (1982), “A Theory of Auctions and Competitive Bidding”, Econometrica, 50(5), 1089–1122. A foundational treatment of auction environments, information and bidding strategy.
  4. EconPort: Winner's Curse. A concise teaching note explaining why the highest estimate is likely to be too high when the underlying value is common.
