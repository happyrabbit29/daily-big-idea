The Principal-Agent
Problem.

Why the person making a decision on your behalf may be rewarded for something different from what you actually want.

FIELD
NOTES
NO. 015
Edition 01513 November 2026Reading time · 7 minutes
The idea in one sentence

Whenever one person delegates a decision to another, different goals and unequal information can make the agent's best move different from the principal's best outcome.

FOLLOW
THE
INCENTIVES
01 / The opening scene

You want a retirement plan. Your fund manager wants a good December.

Imagine you invest for a retirement that is twenty years away. You care about one thing above all: building wealth over time, after fees, without taking risks that could permanently damage the plan. You hire a professional to manage the money. That person makes the daily decisions; you do not.

Now imagine the manager's bonus and job prospects depend heavily on how the fund performs against its benchmark this year. A sensible long-term investment might look awkward for a few quarters. A fashionable stock might make the portfolio look safer relative to peers. Near year-end, the manager has a choice: stay patient with your long horizon, or protect their own short-term record.

The manager does not have to be dishonest for the interests to diverge. They may be intelligent, hardworking and perfectly decent. The problem is that the person bearing the long-term outcome is not necessarily the person making the short-term decision.

This is the principal-agent problem. A principal delegates a task or decision to an agent, but the two may have different objectives. Because the agent usually knows more about their own actions than the principal can observe, writing a contract that guarantees the desired behaviour is difficult.

The useful question is not only “Who made the decision?” It is “What were they rewarded for when they made it?”
02 / The mechanism

Delegation creates a gap. Incentives decide what fills it.

Think of a shareholder and a chief executive, a client and a portfolio manager, a lender and a loan originator, or a homeowner and a property manager. The principal supplies capital, authority or responsibility. The agent gets discretion over how to use it.

Three ingredients make the relationship difficult. First, the principal and agent may want different things. Second, the agent often has better information about effort, judgement and day-to-day choices. Third, the contract cannot describe every future situation or measure every important outcome. A company cannot write a bonus plan that perfectly captures the quality of every decision a CEO will make over five years.

The problem is not that every agent behaves badly. It is that the arrangement can make privately attractive behaviour differ from the outcome the principal would choose with full information and control.

Economists often separate the costs into three parts: monitoring costs paid by the principal to observe and supervise; bonding costs accepted by the agent to reassure the principal, such as guarantees or contractual restrictions; and the residual loss that remains because interests can never be aligned perfectly.

A simple incentive mismatch
Principal wants: durable value over 5 years
Agent is rewarded for: reported profit this quarter
Easy response: delay maintenance and cut training
Hidden cost: weaker operations in future periods

The agent may hit the target without creating much value. The accounting result arrives now; the broken equipment, staff turnover or lost customer loyalty arrives later. A target can be measured precisely and still measure the wrong thing.

03 / Where the idea shows up

The financial crisis had an incentive problem hiding in the pipeline.

In parts of the pre-2008 US mortgage market, some lenders and brokers originated loans that were later packaged into securities and sold to investors. When an originator earned fees mainly for closing more loans, while much of the eventual credit risk travelled elsewhere, the reward for loan volume could be stronger than the reward for careful long-term underwriting.

This is a principal-agent problem in a chain. The borrower, originator, arranger, rating agency, investor and ultimate holder of risk did not all have the same information or incentives. A loan could be profitable for the party arranging it even when its eventual performance was someone else's problem.

That incentive mismatch was one contributor to the crisis, not a complete explanation. Loose lending standards, leverage, securitisation complexity, flawed risk models, rating failures, regulation and a housing-price boom all mattered. The agency lens helps explain one channel through which risk could be created by one party and carried by another.

04 / The same pattern in ordinary business

Follow the reward, and the behaviour becomes less mysterious.

Asset managers

Clients may want long-term, risk-adjusted returns. A manager evaluated on short-term relative performance can be tempted to hug the benchmark, follow crowded trades or avoid a sound but temporarily unpopular position. Career risk becomes part of the portfolio.

Corporate executives

A bonus tied narrowly to annual earnings can encourage delayed spending on maintenance, research or staff development. Deferred compensation, clawbacks and longer measurement periods can help, but only if the metrics reflect real value.

Sales teams

Paying only for signed contracts can reward volume over suitability. Returns, cancellations, customer complaints and repeat business reveal whether the sale was actually good for the company.

Outsourced operations

A vendor paid per service call may have little incentive to prevent faults. A contract that rewards uptime and durable resolution can produce a different kind of effort from one that simply counts completed tickets.

The important detail is that incentives shape behaviour at the margin. A person may care about the principal's goals and still respond to the scorecard, especially when the scorecard determines pay, promotion or continued employment.

05 / What it is not

Not every disappointing outcome is an agency failure.

Different outcomes do not prove bad faith. A manager can make a reasonable decision that turns out badly. Uncertainty, bad luck and genuine differences of judgement exist. The agency problem concerns the structure of the relationship, not a verdict on someone's character.

Incentives can backfire in both directions. Pay tied to short-term results can encourage excessive caution or excessive risk-taking, depending on how rewards and penalties are designed. Stock-based pay may align managers with shareholders, but it can also encourage focus on the share price over the period that matters.

Monitoring is not free. More reporting, audits and approvals can reduce some hidden actions, but they can also slow decisions, crowd out initiative and create a culture of box-ticking. No principal can observe everything an agent does.

Alignment is not identical to control. The goal is not to remove discretion. Delegation exists because the agent often has expertise or time that the principal lacks. A good arrangement gives the agent room to use that expertise while making the important incentives and risks visible.

06 / The practical test

Before judging the decision, inspect the contract behind it.

When analysing a company, fund, service provider or institution, map the relationship before assuming that everyone is working toward the same definition of success.

  1. Who is the principal, and who is the agent?
    Identify who supplies the capital or bears the outcome, and who actually controls the decision.
  2. What is the agent rewarded for?
    Look beyond the mission statement. Read the bonus metrics, fee structure, promotion criteria and contract terms.
  3. What can the agent see or do that the principal cannot easily observe?
    Hidden effort, risk selection, timing, quality and information can matter more than the headline result.
  4. Who carries the downside if the decision fails?
    If the reward is immediate but the cost is delayed or transferred to someone else, the arrangement deserves closer scrutiny.

Better governance does not require assuming the worst about people. It requires designing systems in which doing the right thing is also a sensible thing for the person making the decision.

Further reading / source notes

Follow the trail.

The modern economic treatment of agency costs, moral hazard and the separation of ownership from control developed through work in contract theory and corporate finance.

  1. Michael C. Jensen and William H. Meckling (1976), “Theory of the Firm: Managerial Behavior, Agency Costs and Ownership Structure”, Journal of Financial Economics, 3(4), 305–360. A foundational framework for agency costs and ownership incentives.
  2. Bengt Holmström (1979), “Moral Hazard and Observability”, Bell Journal of Economics, 10(1), 74–91. A classic analysis of incentives when actions cannot be perfectly observed.
  3. Eugene F. Fama and Michael C. Jensen (1983), “Separation of Ownership and Control”, Journal of Law and Economics, 26(2), 301–325. Explores how organisations can control agency problems when decision management and risk-bearing are separated.
  4. Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission (2011), The Financial Crisis Inquiry Report. A broad investigation of the causes of the 2007–09 financial crisis, including mortgage origination, securitisation and risk transfer.
