The Endowment
Effect.

Why owning something can quietly change what we think it is worth, and how to stop “mine” from masquerading as a valuation.

The idea in one sentence

We often demand more to give up something we own than we would willingly pay to acquire the exact same thing.

01 / The opening scene

The mug that suddenly became priceless.

Imagine a room of students. Half are handed an ordinary coffee mug. The other half receive nothing. A few minutes later, everyone is asked to put a price on the same mug: owners name the amount they would accept to sell it; non-owners name the amount they would pay to buy it.

The mugs are identical. Nobody has had time to become emotionally attached to the ceramic. Yet, in a famous set of experiments, the owners generally asked for more than the buyers were prepared to pay. When the researchers ran markets, fewer mugs changed hands than standard economic theory would have predicted.

The object had not changed. The owner's relationship to it had.

This is the endowment effect: the tendency to value something more once it becomes part of what we own. It helps explain why the same person can look at an object as a bargain when buying it and as precious when selling it.

The moment something becomes “mine”, giving it up can feel like losing more than buying it ever felt like gaining.
02 / The mechanism

Ownership moves the mental goalposts.

Before ownership, the mug is one possible purchase among many. After ownership, it becomes part of the current state of the world. Selling it means moving away from that state. The mind can frame that change as a loss, and losses often carry more psychological weight than equivalent gains.

That asymmetry is closely linked to loss aversion, a central idea in behavioural economics. The money received from selling may feel like a gain, but the mug surrendered feels like a loss. The two sides of the trade do not necessarily cancel out in our heads.

WTP = willingness to pay. WTA = willingness to accept. The endowment effect describes a gap that can emerge between the two; it is not a fixed percentage or universal rule.

Willingness to pay (WTP) asks, “How much would I spend to get this?” Willingness to accept (WTA) asks, “How much would I need to be compensated to give this up?” For some goods and situations, WTA can sit well above WTP.

03 / A familiar example

The wine you cannot quite sell.

In a 1991 article, Daniel Kahneman, Jack Knetsch and Richard Thaler described a wine-loving economist who had bought bottles of Bordeaux years earlier at low prices. The wine had appreciated substantially. He would occasionally drink a bottle, but was unwilling to sell one at the current auction price and equally unwilling to buy another at that same price.

That is the puzzle in miniature. If the bottle is worth the auction price to the market, why does the owner neither sell nor buy at that price? The answer may include taste, convenience and personal history. But ownership itself can make surrendering the bottle feel different from acquiring an identical one.

The two-sided price test
Buyer: “Would I pay ₹5,000 for it?”
Owner: “Would I accept ₹5,000 to give it up?”

If the answers are different, ask what explains the gap. Is it a genuine feature of the item, a replacement cost, a transaction cost or simply the discomfort of losing something that is already yours?

04 / Where it shows up

From kitchen cupboards to capital allocation.

Homes and possessions

A homeowner may set a reservation price above what comparable buyers will pay. The house carries memories and a familiar routine for the seller; the buyer sees a property, its location and its alternatives.

Corporate portfolios

A company may hold on to a division, plant or product line that it would not choose to acquire today. Managers can treat an existing asset as part of the organisation's identity and resist the loss involved in selling it.

Investment portfolios

An investor may become attached to shares already held, especially when the position has a personal story. The holding starts to feel like the default, while selling feels like surrendering something rather than reallocating capital.

Negotiations

Buyers and sellers can arrive at very different prices because they are not evaluating the same psychological starting point. Each side may see its own preferred outcome as normal and the other side's proposal as a loss.

For investors, the useful question is not whether a share has fallen below the purchase price. The purchase price is history. The question is whether the business and its expected future cash flows justify owning it today at today's price. The endowment effect can make that reassessment harder.

Be careful not to confuse it with the disposition effect, the tendency studied in finance for investors to sell winners too readily and hold losers too long. The ideas are related through loss aversion and reference points, but they are not interchangeable explanations for every decision to hold or sell.

05 / The limits

Not every attachment is a bias.

Some differences are rational. A seller may face real replacement costs, search costs, taxes, legal restrictions or uncertainty about finding an equivalent item. A concert ticket for a sold-out show is not perfectly interchangeable with cash if the experience matters to its owner.

The effect is not universal. It varies with the good, the person, the market and the way choices are elicited. In some settings, ownership creates little or no gap between WTP and WTA.

Researchers have debated the evidence. Plott and Zeiler (2005) argued that some measured gaps could arise from how experiments are explained and conducted. With extensive training and procedural controls in their experiments, they found no WTP–WTA gap for the goods they tested. That does not erase all evidence for endowment effects; it is a reminder that experimental design and interpretation matter.

Market prices still matter. A seller's emotional reservation price does not make a buyer's alternative disappear. In competitive markets, other owners, substitutes and willing buyers can constrain what an asset is actually worth in exchange.

06 / The practical test

Try the empty-hands exercise.

When you are reluctant to sell, keep the asset exactly the same but mentally remove the fact that you own it. Imagine that you have the equivalent amount in cash instead. Would you use that cash to buy this asset today, at its current price?

If the answer is no, pause before acting. There may be a good reason to keep it, but “because it is already mine” is not an investment thesis.

  1. What would I pay to acquire this today?
    Set aside the price you originally paid and assess the asset on its current merits.
  2. What would genuinely make me reluctant to sell?
    Separate taxes, replacement costs, information advantages and personal value from the simple discomfort of giving something up.
  3. Would I make the same decision if I did not own it?
    If not, identify what ownership has added to the calculation. It may be useful information, or it may be quietly moving the goalposts.
Further reading / source notes

Follow the trail.

The endowment effect is associated with reference-dependent valuation and loss aversion. The research record includes both influential demonstrations and methodological challenges; the size and robustness of the effect depend on context and experimental design.

  1. Daniel Kahneman, Jack L. Knetsch and Richard H. Thaler (1990), “Experimental Tests of the Endowment Effect and the Coase Theorem”, Journal of Political Economy. The classic mug-market experiments.
  2. Daniel Kahneman, Jack L. Knetsch and Richard H. Thaler (1991), “Anomalies: The Endowment Effect, Loss Aversion, and Status Quo Bias”, Journal of Economic Perspectives. A clear account of the idea and its relationship to loss aversion.
  3. Charles R. Plott and Kathryn Zeiler (2005), “The Willingness to Pay–Willingness to Accept Gap, the ‘Endowment Effect,’ Subject Misconceptions, and Experimental Procedures for Eliciting Valuations”, American Economic Review. A methodological challenge to how some experimental gaps are interpreted.
