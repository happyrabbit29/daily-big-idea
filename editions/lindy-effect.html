The Lindy
Effect.

Why the survival of an idea, technology or institution can be evidence about how long it may continue to matter, and why this is not a magic forecasting trick.

The idea in one sentence

For things that do not have a natural expiry date, the longer they have survived, the longer they may be expected to survive.

TIME TESTS
WHAT TIME
CAN DESTROY
01 / The opening scene

A book that refuses to disappear.

Imagine two books on a shelf. One was published last month and has sold a million copies because it is the subject of a viral trend. The other has been read, argued over and reprinted for 300 years. Which one is more likely to still be read a century from now?

The new bestseller may become a classic. But the older book has already passed a long sequence of tests: changing tastes, competing ideas, new technologies, generations of readers and countless chances to be forgotten. Its survival is evidence. Not proof, but evidence.

This intuition is called the Lindy effect. It proposes that, for certain non-perishable things, life expectancy can increase with age. A book, mathematical theorem, language, recipe or institution does not age in the same way a banana does. If it has lasted, it may have qualities that help it keep lasting.

For things that can die of old age, time consumes them. For things that do not, time can be a test they keep passing.
02 / Where the name comes from

From a New York deli to a theory of time.

The name is associated with Lindy's delicatessen in New York, where comedians reportedly discussed how long performers' careers might last. The rough observation was that a comedian who had survived on the circuit for ten years might have a better chance of surviving another ten than a newcomer had of surviving even one.

Writer Albert Goldman described the idea in a 1964 article about the show-business world. Mathematician and writer Benoit Mandelbrot later developed a related discussion of heavy-tailed distributions and the Lindy effect. Nassim Nicholas Taleb popularised the concept in modern discussions of uncertainty and antifragility, especially in Antifragile.

The central distinction is between perishable and non-perishable things. A person, machine battery or loaf of bread has a physical ageing process. A theorem does not become weaker because it is 200 years old. A language does not have a fixed biological lifespan. Their survival depends on continued use, relevance, adaptation and transmission.

03 / The mechanism

Survival is information.

Think of every year as another filter. An idea competes for attention. A technology competes with alternatives. An institution faces wars, regulation, new habits and changing incentives. Many disappear. Those that remain may have accumulated advantages: they solve a recurring problem, are easy to teach, fit existing systems or can adapt without losing their core function.

The effect is strongest when survival reflects repeated exposure to meaningful failure risks, not simply protection from competition.

In a simplified version of the Lindy rule, if something has already lasted for T years, its expected remaining life may also be proportional to T. That is a heuristic, not a universal law of nature. The underlying statistical story is often connected to heavy-tailed or power-law survival distributions, where the risk of disappearance does not behave like a fixed countdown.

04 / A numerical intuition

Why age can change the odds.

A simplified illustration

Suppose a particular class of non-perishable ideas has a survival pattern in which the expected total lifespan, conditional on having already survived, scales roughly with current age. This is the intuition behind the popular rule of thumb:

Expected remaining life ≈ time already survived

An idea that has survived 5 years might, under this simplified model, have roughly 5 expected years remaining. One that has survived 50 years might have roughly 50. The point is not that a 50-year-old idea has a scheduled expiry in 100 years. It is that its age contains information about its ability to persist.

Contrast this with a machine component that wears out at a fairly predictable rate. If its failure risk rises as it gets older, age can be a warning sign rather than a vote of confidence. The category of thing matters before the rule does.

05 / The investor's version

Durability is a competitive advantage.

Investors encounter Lindy-like reasoning when they assess business models, brands, networks and institutions. A company that has navigated several recessions, technological shifts and competitive attacks has demonstrated something a newly fashionable business has not: an ability to survive different environments.

Brands and trust

A brand used across generations may benefit from recognition, distribution, habit and trust. Those are real assets, though they can erode when customer needs or channels change.

Networks and standards

Widely adopted standards become difficult to displace because users, suppliers, software and infrastructure coordinate around them. Their age can reflect network effects and switching costs.

Business models

Recurring customer needs, disciplined capital allocation and the ability to adapt can help a business survive cycles. A long record is a useful starting point for research.

Institutions

Institutions that persist may have earned legitimacy, solved coordination problems or adapted to new conditions. Their history is evidence, not a certificate of permanent competence.

But longevity is not the same as investment quality. A 100-year-old company can be overpriced, poorly governed or structurally disrupted. A younger company can have a genuine advantage that has not yet had time to build a long record. Lindy helps frame questions; it does not replace valuation, cash-flow analysis or competitive research.

06 / Where the idea breaks

Survivors are not automatically winners.

Survivorship bias: We see the businesses, books and institutions that lasted, not the thousands that failed. Looking only at survivors can make persistence look easier and more common than it was.

Artificial protection: A company may survive because of subsidies, monopoly privileges, bailouts or regulation that blocks competition. Its age may reveal political protection more than adaptability.

Changing environments: A solution can be durable in one world and obsolete in another. A typewriter manufacturer could have decades of operating history and still face a technology that changes the category entirely.

Perishable versus non-perishable: Applying Lindy to people, physical assets or products with wear-out mechanisms is a category error. A 90-year-old person does not have a longer expected remaining lifespan simply because they reached 90.

Age is not causation: Longevity may be correlated with quality, but it can also reflect luck, market power, path dependence or a lack of alternatives. Ask what mechanism kept the thing alive.

07 / Three better questions

Use history as evidence, not as a shortcut.

  1. What exactly has survived?
    The brand, the legal entity, the product, the business model or the underlying customer need? Those can have very different histories.
  2. What tests has it actually passed?
    Has it survived genuine competition, technological change and economic stress, or has it been sheltered from them?
  3. What could make the next decade different?
    Identify new technologies, regulation, customer behaviour and substitutes that could break the pattern. History informs the forecast; it does not get to veto new evidence.
Further reading / source notes

Follow the trail.

The modern Lindy effect draws on older ideas about survival distributions and has been popularised in writing about uncertainty. The exact “remaining life equals age” formulation is a heuristic, not a universal empirical law.

  1. Albert Goldman (1964), “Lindy's Law,” The New Republic. An early account connecting the idea to comedians and show business.
  2. Nassim Nicholas Taleb (2012), Antifragile: Things That Gain from Disorder. A popular modern treatment of Lindy and survival under uncertainty.
  3. Benoit B. Mandelbrot and Richard L. Hudson (2004), The (Mis)Behavior of Markets. Background on scaling, heavy tails and why ordinary bell-curve intuition can fail.
  4. Encyclopaedia Britannica, Power law. A concise introduction to the family of distributions often used to describe heavy-tailed phenomena.
