Why the winners we can still see are not a fair sample of everyone who tried, and how the missing failures distort our picture of success.

The idea in one sentence

When we study only the people, companies or funds that survive, we mistake a selected group of winners for the full population that took the risk.

01 / The opening scene

The planes that came back were telling only half the story.

During the Second World War, military analysts wanted to understand where returning aircraft were being damaged. The visible evidence seemed straightforward: inspect the planes that made it home, map the bullet and flak damage, then reinforce the areas with the most holes.

But statistician Abraham Wald, working with the Statistical Research Group at Columbia University, was concerned with a deeper question: what did the data leave out? The aircraft being inspected were, by definition, the ones that had survived their missions. Planes hit in especially vulnerable places might never have returned to be counted.

So the pattern on the surviving aircraft could be misleading. Damage in an area might show that a plane could often survive a hit there. A lack of visible damage might be more worrying if a hit in that area was likely to prevent the aircraft from returning.

Wald's actual work was a series of technical memoranda on estimating aircraft vulnerability from survivor data, not just the neat one-line instruction repeated in popular retellings. The enduring insight is simpler: the evidence you can observe may have been filtered by the very outcome you are trying to understand.

The missing observations are not necessarily random. Some may be missing because they failed.
02 / The mechanism

Survival is a filter, not a neutral sampling process.

Imagine 100 businesses launch with similar ambitions. Ten become durable successes, 20 are acquired, and 70 close. If you study only the ten still operating, you may conclude that their founders all had extraordinary discipline, that their markets were attractive, or that their strategies were obviously superior.

But you have quietly removed most of the comparison group. Perhaps the surviving firms benefited from timing, access to capital, luck or a market tailwind. Perhaps some failed firms made similar decisions but faced different conditions. The survivors alone cannot tell you which factors caused success.

The bias arises when inclusion in the sample depends on survival or another outcome related to what we are trying to measure.

This is survivorship bias. It is a form of selection bias: the observations that remain available are systematically different from the ones that disappeared. The direction and size of the distortion depend on how survival is determined. It often makes success look more common or performance look stronger than it was, but it does not always push estimates in the same direction.

The statistical question
What we observe = outcomes, conditional on survival

That is not automatically the same as the outcomes of the full original group. The missing group matters, especially when its disappearance is related to poor performance, risk or failure.

03 / The market version

The fund league table with the losers erased.

Suppose an analyst compares the long-term returns of mutual funds that exist today. Funds that performed poorly may have been closed or merged over the years. If the database includes only funds still operating at the end of the period, the weakest histories can vanish from the comparison.

The resulting average can look better than the experience of an investor who could actually have chosen from the full set of funds at the start. Academic research has documented this problem in mutual-fund performance studies. Elton, Gruber and Blake examined fund attrition and showed why funds that disappear must be accounted for; later work has explored how the bias varies with sample definitions and fund characteristics.

The same trap appears in equity research. Imagine testing a strategy using today's index constituents and then calculating how those companies performed over the last decade. You have selected the winners that remain in the index today and ignored companies that were removed, acquired or delisted. The backtest is no longer asking, “What would an investor have experienced then?” It is asking, “How did today's survivors do in the past?”

A list of today's winners is not a historical opportunity set.
04 / Beyond finance

Success stories are a dangerously edited genre.

Start-ups

Founder interviews and case studies naturally focus on companies that survived long enough to become interesting. Their habits may be useful, but they do not reveal how often the same habits appeared in companies that failed.

Careers and history

We hear from people who reached the top, not everyone who made similar sacrifices or took similar risks. Their stories can confuse a possible path with a probable one.

There is a subtle danger here. Once a survivor tells a coherent story about why it succeeded, the explanation feels inevitable. But the story is reconstructed after the outcome. The unsuccessful people who made similar choices are no longer in the room to challenge it.

05 / Why it matters

It can turn a fragile strategy into an apparent rule.

For investors, survivorship bias can inflate historical returns, make active managers look more skilled, and make a backtested strategy seem easier to execute than it would have been in real time. It can also distort estimates of volatility, drawdowns and the frequency of failure if distressed or delisted observations are missing.

For businesses, it encourages leaders to copy the visible habits of celebrated companies without studying the much larger set of firms that tried similar things and did not survive. It can make risk-taking look cheap because the costs are recorded in someone else's missing data.

A simple thought experiment
Observed success rate = survivors that succeeded ÷ survivors observed

A genuine success rate needs the full starting population and a clear definition of success. If failures disappear before the count is made, the denominator is wrong before the arithmetic even begins.

06 / Where the idea has limits

Survivors are useful. They are just not the whole story.

Studying survivors is not inherently wrong. If the question is how today's operating companies are investing, the current population may be exactly what you need. The mistake is using that selected sample to make claims about all companies that existed years ago, or about the probability that a new company will succeed.

Nor is every disappearance a failure. A fund may merge for administrative reasons, a company may be acquired at a premium, or a dataset may stop tracking an entity for reasons unrelated to performance. Good analysis records the reason for exit and, where relevant, includes the final return or acquisition value. It also avoids treating every missing observation as if it had the same outcome.

The right fix is to define the original population, preserve historical membership, include closed and delisted entities where possible, and be explicit about how missing data are handled. Sometimes the information simply does not exist. In that case, the honest answer is a range of uncertainty, not a confident average.

07 / The practical takeaway

Ask who is missing before admiring who remains.

Whenever a dataset, success story or investment backtest looks unusually impressive, inspect the process that decided what got included.

  • Rebuild the starting universe: Who was present at the beginning, not just at the end? Include closed funds, delisted stocks, failed projects and acquired businesses where relevant.
  • Understand the exits: Did entities disappear because of poor performance, a merger, a change in reporting or something else? The reason changes the interpretation.
  • Test the selection rule: Would the conclusion still hold if you included the observations that did not survive?
Keep this question

“Am I learning from the whole experiment, or only from the people and companies that were left to tell the story?”
