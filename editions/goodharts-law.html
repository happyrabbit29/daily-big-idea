Goodhart’s Law: The Metric Trap.

Why a useful measure can become a misleading target once people are rewarded for improving the number rather than the thing it was meant to represent.

Edition 002 · 01 NOV 2026 · Reading time 6–8 minutes
The idea in one sentence

When a measure becomes a target, people change their behaviour around the measure, and it often stops being a good measure.

01 / The opening scene

The school that wanted perfect test scores.

Imagine a school judged almost entirely on its students’ exam results. At first, test scores are a reasonable signal: they tell the school something about learning. But once funding, rankings and teacher bonuses depend on those scores, the incentives shift.

Teachers may narrow lessons to likely exam questions. Students may memorise patterns instead of understanding concepts. The score rises, but the underlying goal, learning, may not improve by as much. The metric has been gamed, sometimes deliberately and sometimes simply because people adapt to incentives.

This is the intuition behind Goodhart’s Law, associated with British economist Charles Goodhart. It is a warning about the gap between a proxy and the thing we actually care about.

The number was supposed to describe the goal. Then the goal became getting the number.
02 / Why the metric breaks

A proxy is not the whole thing.

Many important outcomes are hard to measure directly. Customer satisfaction, employee productivity, credit quality and student learning are complex. Organisations therefore use proxies: response times, sales, output per hour, test scores or default rates.

While the proxy is only one input into judgement, it can be useful. But once rewards and penalties depend heavily on it, people optimise what is counted. They may shift effort away from unmeasured dimensions, select easier cases, change classifications or find loopholes.

Call-centre example

Target: Reduce average call time

Agents learn to end calls quickly. Average handling time improves, but customers call back because their problems were not solved. The dashboard looks healthier while the customer experience gets worse.

A better system might track resolution on first contact, repeat calls, customer feedback and quality audits alongside call duration.

03 / The investor’s dashboard

When the KPI becomes the business.

A company that rewards sales teams only for revenue may encourage aggressive discounting or low-quality customer acquisition. A bank focused only on near-term loan growth may underweight credit standards. A fund manager judged only on quarterly performance may take risks that look attractive over a short measurement window but damage long-term returns.

Revenue growth

Can be inflated through discounts, channel stuffing or acquisitions that destroy value. Pair it with margins, cash conversion and customer retention.

Employee utilisation

High utilisation can look efficient while leaving no room for training, collaboration or unexpected work. Pair it with quality and sustainable workload.

Low defect rates

Teams may under-report defects or avoid difficult projects. Use independent checks and track near misses, not just recorded failures.

Portfolio returns

A short-term benchmark can encourage closet indexing or excessive risk. Examine performance across cycles, fees and risk exposures.

04 / Better measurement

Make gaming harder, not measurement impossible.

The answer is not to abandon metrics. Without measurement, decisions can become vague and accountability weak. Instead, use multiple indicators, audit the underlying activity, keep qualitative judgement in the loop and change measures when behaviour starts to distort them.

Goodhart’s Law is often quoted as if every target inevitably becomes useless. That is too strong. Some targets remain informative, especially when they are hard to manipulate and closely tied to the outcome. The key is to expect adaptation: people respond to what an organisation rewards.

Think it through

Three questions to carry with you.

  1. What important outcome is this metric only trying to approximate?
  2. If someone were rewarded entirely on this number, how could they improve it without improving the real outcome?
  3. Which second metric or independent check would expose that behaviour?
