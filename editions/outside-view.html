The Outside
View.

Why the most useful forecast may begin by looking away from the project in front of you, and asking how similar projects actually turned out.

The idea in one sentence

When a forecast is built around a unique story, anchor it to the actual distribution of outcomes from comparable cases before trusting the details of the plan.

01 / The opening scene

A beautiful building. A very difficult estimate.

In 1957, the design for the Sydney Opera House was selected. The project was meant to become a landmark for a young country. Its original estimate was about £3.5 million, and the target opening date was Australia Day in 1963. The drawings were striking. The ambition was enormous. The plan, on paper, looked like a building project.

Then the site began to answer questions the drawings had not. The geology at Bennelong Point was not what had been assumed. The foundations needed substantial work that had not been budgeted. The eventual weight and anchoring requirements of the roof were still uncertain. Construction began before critical design questions had been settled, and the early momentum made it harder to stop and rethink the sequence.

The Opera House opened on 20 October 1973. The final cost was about A$102 million; the New South Wales archival guide notes that its figure excludes the organ and car park. The result became one of the world's best-known buildings. It was also a warning about treating a project's own story as a forecast.

A plan explains how success is supposed to happen. A reference class asks how often plans like this have actually succeeded.

This is the difference between the inside view and the outside view. The inside view studies this project's design, team, suppliers, milestones and special advantages. The outside view steps back and looks at the recorded outcomes of a relevant group of similar projects.

02 / The mechanism

Trade the confident story for a distribution.

People naturally build forecasts from the details in front of them. That can be useful. It can also make a new project feel more predictable than it is. Teams know the schedule they have drawn, the efficiencies they expect and the reasons their project is different. They may know much less about the full range of ways comparable projects have gone wrong.

Reference class forecasting gives the outside view a practical form. Rather than trying to imagine every delay or cost shock, it uses the actual record of a suitable group of completed projects as the starting point for the forecast.

Reference class forecastingThree steps, then an adjustment
01 / DEFINEChoose the classFind completed projects similar in type, scale, complexity and setting.
02 / MEASUREBuild the distributionCollect actual cost, schedule or demand outcomes, not just original promises.
03 / LOCATEPlace the new projectAsk where this project belongs in the historical range of outcomes.
04 / ADJUSTDemand evidenceMove away from the class baseline only for specific, verifiable reasons.

The reference class should be broad enough to contain a meaningful sample, but narrow enough that its projects are genuinely comparable.

The method is associated with the work of Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky on the outside view, and was developed into a practical project-forecasting method by Bent Flyvbjerg and other researchers. It is not a claim that every new project will match the average. It is a way to stop a persuasive project narrative from becoming the only evidence in the room.

03 / The evidence

What happened to the projects before yours?

A landmark study by Bent Flyvbjerg, Mette Skamris Holm and Søren Buhl examined 258 transport infrastructure projects across 20 countries. In that historical sample, average construction-cost escalation differed materially by project type:

Historical reference class / 258 projects

Rail projects

44.7% average cost escalation across 58 projects.

Bridges and tunnels

33.8% average cost escalation across 33 projects.

Road projects

20.4% average cost escalation across 167 projects.

The warning

The standard deviations were wide. The average describes a group, not the fate of every individual project.

Figures are from the study's constant-price estimates. They are historical sample averages, not universal uplifts for projects being assessed today.

Notice what the numbers do not say. They do not say that every rail project will overrun by 44.7%, or that roads are safe because their average is lower. They say that the type of project matters, and that the spread around an average matters too. Combining rail, roads and tunnels into one generic “infrastructure” benchmark would hide information that should change the forecast.

It is also why a distribution is more useful than a single number. A central estimate helps with the base case. A more adverse percentile can help size contingency and test financing resilience. The right percentile depends on the decision, the sponsor's risk tolerance and the consequences of running out of money.

04 / A worked example

How a benchmark changes the investment case.

Imagine a utility proposing a new transmission project. Management presents a budget of $1 billion. The internal model assumes the line is energised on schedule, so the forecast cash flows begin when planned. The equity story depends on the project being delivered close to that budget.

Now suppose the analyst builds a reference class of genuinely comparable completed projects. For this illustrative example only, assume the class has a median cost overrun of 25% and an 80th-percentile overrun of 50%.

Illustrative arithmetic / not an industry benchmark
$1.0bn budget × 1.25 = $1.25bn median outcome
$1.0bn budget × 1.50 = $1.50bn 80th-percentile outcome

The analyst now has a different question. Is management's $1 billion estimate supported by a contract structure, route, permit status, contractor record and engineering maturity that genuinely place this project near the favourable end of the class? Or is the model quietly assuming that the project will be exceptional because the investment case needs it to be?

If the project still earns attractive returns at a more realistic cost and a later commissioning date, the thesis may be robust. If a modest overrun destroys the return, the forecast is fragile even before the first excavator arrives.

05 / Why investors should care

Capex forecasts are valuation assumptions in disguise.

For an investor, a project budget is not just a line in management's presentation. It influences future depreciation, financing needs, free cash flow, production capacity and the date at which expected revenue can begin. A delay can hurt twice: the company spends for longer while the cash flows arrive later.

For capital-intensive companies

Compare mines, refineries, pipelines, power plants, telecom roll-outs and transmission networks with completed projects of similar scale and complexity. Test both the capex number and the promised commissioning date.

For management quality

Track forecast accuracy across projects. A management team that repeatedly delivers below budget and on time may deserve a different assessment from one whose plans repeatedly migrate.

Reference classes can also improve revenue forecasts. A new plant's nameplate capacity is not the same as its first-year output. Compare actual ramp-up curves from similar facilities, customer adoption rates from comparable launches, or realised traffic against forecasts for similar transport assets.

HM Treasury's optimism-bias guidance makes the same practical point for public appraisal: where robust project-specific evidence is missing, adjust estimates of cost, benefits and duration using data from past or similar projects, then account for the characteristics of the project being assessed.

06 / The limits

The comparison can be wrong too.

The outside view is not a magic penalty that should be pasted onto every budget. It depends on the quality of the comparison.

  • Class selection is judgment. A class that is too broad mixes unlike projects. A class that is too narrow may contain too few observations. Either can produce a misleading benchmark.
  • History may not match today. New technology, regulation, financing conditions, labour markets or supply chains can change the distribution. Old data should not be treated as timeless.
  • Scope must be consistent. One project's final cost may include land, financing and owner costs while another's does not. Nominal and real costs, approved scope and final scope must be defined carefully.
  • Unique projects still exist. A genuinely novel undertaking may have no close reference class. That does not make it predictable; it makes the range of uncertainty wider and the case for staging, contingencies and independent review stronger.

The inside view still matters. Detailed engineering, site surveys and contractor evidence can justify a project-specific adjustment. The discipline is to make that adjustment explicit and testable rather than letting every special feature become a reason to ignore the historical record.

07 / Put it to work

Five questions before you believe the forecast.

  1. What is the reference class of completed projects that is genuinely comparable in type, scale and complexity?
  2. What did those projects actually cost, how long did they take, and how quickly did they reach planned output?
  3. Where does management's estimate sit in that distribution: near the median, the optimistic tail or the adverse tail?
  4. What verifiable evidence places this project in a better part of the distribution than its peers?
  5. Does the investment still make sense under a less flattering cost, timing and ramp-up assumption?
A forecast becomes more credible when it can explain why it deserves to differ from the evidence, not merely why its own plan looks convincing.

The practical takeaway: Before accepting a project budget, revenue ramp or delivery date, ask for the comparison set and the actual outcomes. Start with the distribution. Then let project-specific evidence earn its way into the forecast.
