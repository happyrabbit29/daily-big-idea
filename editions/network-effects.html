Why a product can become more valuable as more people use it, and how that simple feedback loop can turn a useful service into a formidable business.

The idea in one sentence

A network effect exists when each additional participant makes the product or platform more useful to other participants, creating a feedback loop that can strengthen with scale.

01 / The opening scene

The first fax machine was an expensive paperweight.

Imagine buying a fax machine in a world where nobody else owns one. The machine works perfectly. It can scan a page, convert it into a signal and send it down a telephone line. There is just one problem: nobody is there to receive it.

The second machine changes the situation. Now two owners can exchange documents. The third creates more possible connections. As offices, suppliers, banks and customers join, the service becomes useful to a much larger group. The value is no longer just the machine's engineering. It is the people on the other end.

This is the basic intuition behind a network effect: the usefulness of a product to one participant rises as more relevant participants join. The product is partly the network itself.

The same logic appears in messaging apps, payment cards, marketplaces, professional networks and software ecosystems. A telephone is useful because other people have telephones. A marketplace is useful because buyers can find sellers and sellers can find buyers.

A good product solves a problem. A network can make the solution more valuable every time another person joins.
02 / The mechanism

Growth can feed on itself.

Network effects create a loop. More participants attract more participants because the service becomes more useful, which makes it easier to attract the next group. But the loop is not automatic. Early on, a platform may be almost useless because neither side has enough people to make a match.

The loop strengthens only if new participation adds relevant value and the platform can preserve trust, quality and a good user experience.

There are two common forms:

Direct network effects

The same type of user makes the product more valuable to other users. More friends on a messaging service, for example, mean more people you can reach.

Indirect or cross-side effects

One group makes the service more valuable to another group. More merchants make a payment card more useful to cardholders; more cardholders make it more attractive to merchants.

Two-sided platforms have a particular coordination problem: buyers want sellers, while sellers want buyers. A platform often has to solve one side of the market first, sometimes by subsidising it, narrowing its geography or focusing on one category until enough activity exists for the loop to begin.

03 / The business version

Why a marketplace can feel empty at 10,000 users and indispensable at 100,000.

Picture a local home-rental marketplace. A guest opens the app and sees three listings, all far from the area they want. They leave. A host joins, sees little demand and stops updating the listing. The next guest sees stale options and leaves too. The platform has users, but not yet a useful network.

Now imagine the company concentrates on one city rather than launching everywhere. It recruits hosts in a few neighbourhoods, improves listing quality and makes booking dependable. Guests begin finding relevant places. Hosts receive enquiries and keep their calendars current. More reliable supply attracts more guests; more guests make hosting worthwhile.

The important variable is not the headline number of registered accounts. It is density of useful interactions: can the right participant find the right counterpart, at the right time, with enough trust to complete the exchange?

The investor's lens
Network strength ≠ total sign-ups

Look for repeat usage, successful matches, retention by cohort, transaction frequency and improving acquisition economics. A million dormant accounts do not necessarily create a network. A smaller group that repeatedly finds value in one another may.

04 / Why it matters

The moat may be the relationships, not the feature.

A competitor can copy a screen, cut a subscription price or reproduce a feature. It is harder to reproduce a dense network of trusted participants, established habits, useful data and repeat interactions. This can create a reinforcing advantage: the incumbent offers more choice or reach, which improves retention, which supports further investment in the service.

For an investor, that possibility changes the questions worth asking. Instead of asking only whether the product is good, ask whether each new user improves the experience for existing users. Is the network local or global? Does activity create liquidity? Do users multi-home across rival platforms? What would make them leave, and how quickly could a competitor reach critical density?

Payment networks illustrate the cross-side logic. Cardholders value broad merchant acceptance; merchants value access to cardholders. Neither side's participation can be understood in isolation. The network's value comes from coordinating both.

05 / The caveat

More is not always better.

Network effects are powerful, but they are not magic and they do not guarantee a winner-takes-all outcome.

Networks can get worse

Congestion, spam, fraud, low-quality listings and poor moderation can make a larger network less useful. Growth can create costs as well as value.

Users can belong to many

If customers and suppliers can use several platforms at once, or switch easily, the incumbent may have less pricing power than its scale suggests.

Subsidies can disguise weakness

Discounts and incentives can buy activity without creating durable engagement. When subsidies disappear, the supposed flywheel may stop turning.

Scale is not the same as a moat

Regulation, interoperability, changing habits or a better technology can weaken an established network. Local density may matter more than global user counts.

You may encounter Metcalfe's Law, the heuristic that the number of possible connections in a network can grow roughly with the square of its participants. It is a useful way to picture why connections matter, not a universal valuation formula. Real users do not connect to everyone, connections differ in quality, and value is rarely uniform.

A network effect is not “we are big.” It is “our size makes the service better, and that improvement gives people a reason to stay.”
06 / The practical takeaway

Test the loop, not the slogan.

  1. Who makes the product more valuable to whom?
    Identify the participant groups and the specific benefit that flows between them.
  2. Where does density matter?
    Look for the geography, category or use case where enough relevant activity makes matching reliable.
  3. Is the loop visible in behaviour?
    Check repeat transactions, retention, successful matches and organic referrals, not just registrations or app downloads.
  4. What could break it?
    Test multi-homing, switching costs, trust, congestion, regulation and the economics once incentives are reduced.

When evaluating a platform, draw the loop in plain language. If you cannot explain how one additional active participant makes the experience better for someone else, you may be looking at a growth story rather than a network effect.
