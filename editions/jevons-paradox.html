Jevons Paradox: The Efficiency Trap.

Why making a resource cheaper to use can sometimes increase total consumption rather than reduce it.

Edition 003 · 02 NOV 2026 · Reading time 6–8 minutes
The idea in one sentence

Efficiency lowers the cost of using a resource. If that makes people use it much more, total consumption can rise even while each use becomes more efficient.

01 / The opening scene

The coal-saving machine that helped sell more coal.

In 19th-century Britain, engineers improved the efficiency of steam engines. A more efficient engine needed less coal to produce a unit of work. It seemed obvious that better engines would reduce coal consumption.

But economist William Stanley Jevons argued in 1865 that efficiency could make steam power cheaper, encouraging factories, transport and industry to use more of it. The improved technology did not simply replace old demand. It expanded the range of activities for which coal-powered energy was economical.

This is Jevons Paradox: an efficiency improvement can increase total resource consumption when the resulting increase in demand outweighs the resource saved per unit.

Make something cheaper to use, and people may find many more reasons to use it.
02 / The arithmetic

Efficiency per unit is not total consumption.

Total resource use depends on two things: how much resource each unit of activity consumes, and how much activity takes place. Efficiency improves the first. The lower effective cost can increase the second.

A simple numerical example

Total energy = energy per task × number of tasks

Imagine a service initially uses 10 units of energy per task and people perform 100 tasks. Total use is 1,000 units. A technology cuts energy per task by 40%, to 6 units. If usage rises only to 120 tasks, total use falls to 720.

But if the lower cost causes usage to rise to 200 tasks, total use becomes 1,200 units. Each task is more efficient, yet total consumption is 20% higher than before.

03 / Three routes to rebound

Where the extra demand comes from.

Direct rebound

The same user consumes more of the now-cheaper service: driving more kilometres in a fuel-efficient car, for example.

Indirect rebound

Money saved on the efficient service is spent elsewhere, creating additional demand for energy or materials in other sectors.

Economy-wide effects

Lower costs can change prices, production methods, investment and the structure of the economy, expanding markets that previously could not exist.

New uses

Efficiency can make entirely new applications economical. Cheap computing, for instance, enables workloads that would once have been prohibitively expensive.

04 / Why it matters now

The energy transition has a rebound question.

More efficient lighting reduces electricity needed per hour of illumination. But cheaper lighting may encourage brighter spaces, longer operating hours and more installations. Efficient vehicles use less fuel per kilometre, but lower running costs can encourage more travel or larger vehicles.

Digital technology offers another example. More efficient data processing can reduce energy per computation, while the falling cost of computation expands demand for streaming, AI workloads, cloud storage and connected devices.

None of this means efficiency is pointless. Rebound is not always large enough to erase the savings, and efficiency can reduce costs, emissions and resource use substantially. The size of the rebound depends on the technology, price response, income effects, market structure and policy environment.

05 / The policy lesson

Efficiency needs a boundary condition.

If the goal is to reduce total emissions or resource use, efficiency standards may need to work alongside carbon pricing, clean energy supply, infrastructure planning or absolute resource limits. Measure the final outcome, not only energy consumed per unit of output.

Jevons Paradox is also distinct from the broader rebound effect. Rebound describes how some expected savings are offset by increased use. The paradoxical case is when the rebound is large enough that total resource consumption rises above its original level.

Think it through

Three questions to carry with you.

  1. What is becoming cheaper because of the efficiency improvement?
  2. Could lower costs increase use, create new applications or free up spending elsewhere?
  3. Is the real objective lower resource use per unit, or lower total resource use?
