Cycle.

Why the industries that look most attractive at the top of a boom can become the worst places to invest, and how following the money can reveal the next shift in competitive returns.

The idea in one sentence

High returns attract new investment; that investment creates capacity and competition, which can destroy the very returns that attracted the capital in the first place.

01 / The opening scene

The ships arrived after the party ended.

In a shipping boom, a vessel owner sees strong freight rates and full ships. The obvious move seems simple: order more vessels. Shipyards receive orders, banks finance them, and investors celebrate a growing global trade story. But a large ship is not an app update. It can take years to design, finance and build.

By the time the new ships arrive, the world that justified the orders may have changed. Demand can slow, trade can shrink and hundreds of competitors can receive their own long-awaited vessels at roughly the same time. Each operator now has more capacity to sell into a market that needs less of it. The industry has turned yesterday's prosperity into tomorrow's price competition.

This was not just a neat hypothetical. UNCTAD reported that global merchant fleets received a record 117 million deadweight tonnes of new vessel capacity in 2009, against about 33 million tonnes of demolitions and other withdrawals. New deliveries were 42% higher than in 2008, largely because orders had been placed before the downturn. The result was oversupply that helped keep freight rates depressed even as older ships were scrapped at a sharply higher pace.

A real-world capital cycle · Shipping, 2008–10
New capacity delivered in 2009: 117m dwt
Capacity withdrawn: about 33m dwt
New deliveries vs. 2008: +42%

The order book was a delayed echo of the boom. The ships arriving in 2009 reflected decisions made when demand and freight rates looked very different.

The key point is not that shipowners were foolish. Each company could have had a rational reason to expand. The problem was collective: when many competitors respond to the same attractive returns, the combined investment changes the economics of the whole industry.

The industry can be most profitable just before it becomes most crowded.
02 / The mechanism

Profit attracts capital. Capital attracts competition.

The capital cycle is a way of reading an industry from the supply side. Instead of asking only how quickly demand might grow, it asks how existing profits will change the amount of money, equipment and capacity competing to serve that demand.

The loop is simple, but the timing is not. High prices and strong returns invite expansion. New projects take time to arrive, so current earnings can stay strong while future supply is already being built. When that capacity starts operating, competitors fight harder for customers. Prices, utilisation and returns on capital come under pressure. Eventually, weak economics cause companies to cancel projects, cut spending, sell assets, close plants or scrap equipment. Supply growth slows, and the surviving businesses may regain pricing power.

The five stagesOne cycle · Different industries, different speeds
01 / SCARCITYReturns riseDemand outruns available capacity. Prices, margins and returns improve.
02 / RESPONSECapital rushes inCompetitors announce plants, fleets, mines and new projects.
03 / ARRIVALSupply catches upDelayed projects commission. Utilisation and pricing power weaken.
04 / RETRENCHMENTCapital leavesCapex is cut. Weak assets close, are sold or are retired.
05 / RESETReturns recoverWith less new supply, disciplined survivors may earn better returns again.

This is a framework, not a clock. Stages can overlap, reverse or last many years. A new technology, a demand shock or a policy change can alter the path.

There is a crucial difference between a company's growth and an industry's attractiveness. One company can grow quickly by taking market share from rivals. But if every competitor adds capacity at once, the industry may grow in volume while returns deteriorate. Revenue growth is not the same thing as value creation.

For investors, the important variable is often the return on the next rupee of capital. If new projects earn less than the cost of financing them, expansion can increase reported sales while destroying economic value. Strong current returns can be a signal of future entry, not proof that those returns will persist.

03 / When the supply response changes the price

Oil showed how quickly investment can rewrite the market.

The U.S. shale expansion provides another example. Higher oil prices and improvements in drilling and completion techniques encouraged producers to invest. From the start of 2010 to the end of 2014, U.S. crude production rose from roughly 5.4 million barrels per day to around 9.5 million. The European Central Bank's 2017 review notes that shale oil production trebled between January 2011 and mid-2014.

Then the supply story met a less forgiving market. Oil prices fell from about $108 a barrel in mid-2014 to below $30 in early 2016. The growth in U.S. production was an important part of the supply glut, but it was not the only cause: OPEC's policy choices, expected Iranian exports, weaker demand prospects and a stronger dollar also mattered. The lesson is about the mechanism, not a one-factor explanation of a complex price collapse.

A second real-world example · U.S. shale

Supply response

U.S. crude output rose by about 76% from early 2010 to the end of 2014, while shale output trebled between January 2011 and mid-2014.

Market repricing

Brent crude fell from around $108 per barrel in mid-2014 to below $30 in early 2016 as supply and demand conditions shifted.

Historical figures from ECB and IMF analysis. The oil-price decline had several interacting causes; shale was a major supply-side contributor, not a complete explanation.

Notice the lag. The investment decisions were made when the economics looked attractive. The additional barrels arrived into a market where the balance was changing. The price did not care that the original capital budgets had been approved using a different oil-price assumption.

This is why capital-cycle analysis is especially useful in industries with long project lead times: energy, mining, chemicals, shipping, airlines, steel, paper and semiconductors. Capacity can be planned in one market and delivered into another.

04 / The investor's lens

Follow the projects, not just the earnings estimates.

A conventional industry forecast may begin with demand: electric vehicles need batteries, cities need power, data centres need electricity, and economies need more materials. Those demand stories can be true. The missing question is how much supply will be built in response, by whom, and at what cost.

Imagine two industries with equally attractive demand forecasts. In the first, competitors announce a wave of new capacity, secure financing and place equipment orders. In the second, companies are cancelling projects, retiring old assets and returning cash rather than expanding. The first industry may report faster growth for a while. The second may offer better future pricing and returns if demand remains resilient.

A practical research screen
  1. How much capacity is already committed?
    Look beyond current capex to projects under construction, equipment orders, announced expansions and the industry's order book. The announced pipeline can matter more than last year's spending.
  2. When will that capacity arrive?
    Match commissioning dates against realistic demand growth. A project that arrives after the demand window has passed can turn a good forecast into an oversupply problem.
  3. How full are existing assets?
    Track utilisation, inventory, lead times, pricing and capacity additions. High utilisation can support pricing, but it can also trigger the next investment wave.
  4. Who can survive a downturn?
    Compare balance sheets, unit costs, liquidity, maintenance requirements and access to funding. The lowest-cost, best-capitalised operators may emerge stronger when weaker capacity exits.
  5. Are returns protected from new entrants?
    Check cost advantages, scarce permits, network infrastructure, technology, customer switching costs and capital discipline. High returns are more durable when entry is genuinely difficult.

The best opportunities are not automatically in industries with low capex. They are often in businesses where investment has been rationally restrained, competitors have withdrawn, or a durable advantage prevents new capital from earning an acceptable return. The analyst is trying to understand the supply response before it is fully visible in the income statement.

This is the approach popularised for investors by Marathon Asset Management and collected in Edward Chancellor's Capital Returns: look past the growth-versus-value label and follow the trail of investment. The book's examples range across industries, including global brewers and semiconductors, to show how capital spending and competitive structure shape long-run returns.

05 / The hidden distinction

More demand does not always mean better returns.

Suppose demand for a product grows 10% a year. That sounds wonderful. But if capacity grows 20%, competitors may be forced to cut prices to keep plants running. The market becomes bigger while the return on each factory falls. Customers win from lower prices; shareholders may not.

Now reverse the situation. Demand grows only 3%, but the industry has stopped adding capacity. Older plants close, the order book shrinks and the remaining operators face fewer competitors. Pricing and utilisation may improve even without spectacular end-market growth.

Supply outruns demand. Customers gain choice, prices soften and returns can fall despite expanding sales.

Modest growth with discipline

Demand +3%

Utilisation may tighten. If the industry can serve demand without a capex race, returns may improve.

The direction of returns depends on costs, imports, inventories, technology, regulation and the starting level of capacity. These simple growth rates only illustrate the supply-demand mechanism.

This is not an argument to avoid every high-growth industry. It is an argument to separate demand growth from the competitive economics of supplying it. The investor who asks only “How large can this market become?” is leaving out the part that determines who captures the value.

06 / The caveat

Not every extra factory is a mistake.

Demand can outrun supply

New capacity may be absorbed faster than expected. A wave of investment does not guarantee oversupply if demand grows even faster or if old assets retire at the same time.

Capacity can be an option

Spare capacity can create flexibility: a company may accept the cost of idle assets to respond quickly when demand surprises on the upside. In some settings, this option value can justify excess capacity.

Technology changes the curve

A new process can make old plants uneconomic, shift the industry's cost curve or create a new product category. Yesterday's capacity count may no longer measure tomorrow's competitive supply.

Capital cycles are not clocks

Projects can be delayed, cancelled or made obsolete. Regulation, permits, geopolitics, financing and geology can prevent capital from entering even when returns look attractive. The framework helps organise questions; it does not call market tops and bottoms.

Research on investment also cautions against treating all spare capacity as waste. Real-options analysis shows that, under certain conditions, excess capacity can be valuable because it gives a firm the ability to expand output when demand is favourable. A high-return industry can also defend its economics through scarce assets, strong brands, patents, network infrastructure or a cost advantage that new entrants cannot copy.

And low investment is not automatically bullish. It may reflect a market in structural decline, poor management, weak balance sheets or a technology that is making the product irrelevant. A capital shortage only helps existing producers when there is still enough demand for what they sell.

07 / The practical takeaway

Ask who is building tomorrow's competition.

When an industry looks unusually profitable, resist the urge to extrapolate the current margin. Map the new capacity being financed and built. Estimate when it will arrive. Compare that pipeline with plausible demand growth, asset retirements and the cost positions of the likely survivors.

Then look for businesses that can protect returns when the cycle turns: low-cost operators, disciplined capital allocators, scarce assets and strong balance sheets. A company does not need the fastest growth rate to be the best investment. It needs a path to returns that competitors cannot easily copy away.

The capital cycle changes the question from “How much can this industry grow?” to “What will all that growth do to the returns on the capital chasing it?”
