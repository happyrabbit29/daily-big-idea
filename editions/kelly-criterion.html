The idea in one sentence

The Kelly Criterion chooses the fraction of wealth to risk that maximises expected long-run logarithmic growth, given the probabilities and pay-offs you believe are real.

01 / The opening scene

A winning bet can still be a terrible bet.

Imagine a coin that lands heads 60% of the time. A friend offers even money: stake ₹100, and a win earns ₹100 profit; a loss costs your ₹100 stake. You have discovered a real edge. The obvious temptation is to bet as much as possible. After all, the odds are in your favour.

But the coin does not promise to behave politely. It can land tails twice, three times, or five times in a row. Bet your entire bankroll and one unlucky flip ends the experiment. Bet half and a rough streak can leave you so depleted that recovery becomes difficult. The puzzle is not whether the bet is attractive. It is how much of your capital should be exposed to it.

In 1956, Bell Labs researcher John L. Kelly Jr. published a paper connecting information theory with the rate at which capital could grow through a sequence of favourable bets. The resulting rule became known as the Kelly Criterion. It later found an audience among professional gamblers, sports bettors and investors, including mathematician and investor Edward O. Thorp.

The set-up · Hypothetical coin with an edge
Win probability = 60% · Loss probability = 40%
Net odds = 1 : 1 · Starting bankroll = ₹10,000

The expected profit on each rupee staked is positive. But a positive expected return does not tell you whether to stake 1%, 20% or 100% of your capital.

The Kelly insight is uncomfortable because it separates two questions that people often bundle together: Do I have an edge? and How much should I risk? A correct answer to the first does not rescue a reckless answer to the second.

Being right about the direction is not enough. You also have to be right about the size.
02 / The mechanism

Compounding cares about the fraction, not just the forecast.

Suppose you stake a fraction f of your wealth on each independent, even-money bet. If you win, wealth becomes 1 + f times its previous level. If you lose, it becomes 1 − f. Over many rounds, those multipliers compound.

Kelly chooses the fraction that maximises the expected logarithm of wealth after each round. For a win probability p and loss probability q = 1 − p, the even-money formula is:

The Kelly calculationOne win / loss outcome
f* = p − q = 2p − 1
g(f) = p × ln(1 + f) + q × ln(1 − f)

The first expression gives the optimal fraction for an even-money bet. The second is expected log growth at any chosen fraction. For unequal odds, the formula changes; with net odds b, the binary-bet fraction is (bp − q) / b when the edge is positive.

In our coin example, p is 0.60 and q is 0.40. The Kelly fraction is therefore 20%. With ₹10,000, the model says to risk ₹2,000, not the full ₹10,000. A win takes the bankroll to ₹12,000; a loss takes it to ₹8,000.

Why 20%? Because every round changes the base from which the next round compounds. A 20% loss needs a 25% gain just to get back to the starting point. A 50% loss needs a 100% gain. The deeper the hole, the harder the climb.

Same edge · Different position sizes

Half Kelly · 10% stake

Expected log growth is about 1.50% per round. A win turns ₹10,000 into ₹11,000; a loss leaves ₹9,000.

Full Kelly · 20% stake

Expected log growth is about 2.01% per round, the maximum under these assumptions. A win gives ₹12,000; a loss leaves ₹8,000.

Double Kelly · 40% stake

Expected log growth falls to about −0.24% per round, even though the expected rupee value after one bet rises by 8%. The arithmetic average is being pulled up by large wins; the compounding experience is damaged by the larger losses.

Illustrative mathematics, not a forecast. The calculations assume a known 60% win probability, even odds, repeated independent bets and no fees or taxes.

This is the geometric-versus-arithmetic distinction. The average of possible wealth outcomes can look attractive while the typical compounded path is much less forgiving. Kelly optimises the long-run growth rate of wealth under its assumptions; it does not promise a smooth ride or the highest balance at every interim date.

03 / The quiet power of restraint

Why the optimum is not “as much as you can.”

At small position sizes, increasing the stake makes better use of a genuine edge. Past the optimum, the extra exposure does more harm than good. Each additional rupee at risk magnifies the damage from an adverse outcome, and losses reduce the capital available for future opportunities.

There is another subtlety. The Kelly fraction is not a measure of how confident you feel. It is the output of a model: probabilities, pay-offs and available capital go in; a position size comes out. Change the assumptions and the answer changes. A 60% chance of winning at even odds is not the same opportunity as a 60% chance of winning when the payoff is only half the amount you stand to lose.

For a single binary bet with net odds b, the full-Kelly fraction is (bp − q) / b, provided that this is positive and the model's payoff structure applies. If the estimated edge disappears after costs, the appropriate Kelly stake is zero. A beautiful formula cannot manufacture an advantage that is not there.

Kelly is a position-sizing rule, not a machine for finding profitable ideas.
04 / From the casino table to the portfolio

The same problem appears in investing, with messier inputs.

Edward Thorp helped bring the criterion into practical use in blackjack and later explored its application to securities markets. The shared question was always about sizing: once an opportunity has positive expected value, how much capital should be committed without sacrificing the ability to keep playing?

For an investor, a stock or strategy can have a positive expected return and still deserve a small weight. The return estimate may be uncertain; the downside may be severe; the position may be highly correlated with the rest of the portfolio. A portfolio with several apparently separate bets can, in a crisis, behave like one oversized bet if they all depend on the same interest rate, commodity price, funding market or economic cycle.

In a simplified portfolio model, growth-optimal weights depend not only on expected excess returns but also on volatility and the covariance between assets. That is why the Kelly idea cannot be reduced to “put 20% into every investment with a good story.” Correlations, leverage, liquidity, financing terms and portfolio constraints all change the calculation.

A portfolio thought experiment

A fund manager has three attractive ideas: an oil producer, an oil-services company and a shipping business. On paper, each has its own catalyst. But all three may be exposed to the same underlying oil-price shock. Treating the ideas as independent can dramatically understate the risk of a simultaneous loss.

A Kelly-style process forces the manager to ask how each position changes the growth and risk of the whole portfolio, rather than sizing each thesis in isolation.

The important lesson for company analysis is that a high expected return does not automatically justify a large allocation. Valuation, balance-sheet resilience, uncertainty in forecasts and portfolio overlap all matter. Position sizing is where a good research thesis meets the reality of limited capital.

05 / The investor's checklist

Before you size the opportunity, interrogate the edge.

  1. What is the actual edge?
    State the probability and payoff that make the opportunity attractive. Separate evidence from conviction, and do not confuse a compelling narrative with a measurable advantage.
  2. How fragile are the inputs?
    Ask how much the probability, expected return or downside changes under less favourable assumptions. A small error in the estimated edge can produce a large error in the recommended stake.
  3. What else in the portfolio loses at the same time?
    Look through sector, factor, currency, funding and counterparty exposures. Correlation often rises precisely when liquidity and diversification are most valuable.
  4. Can the portfolio survive the path, not just the destination?
    Model drawdowns, leverage, margin calls, redemptions and liquidity needs. A theoretically optimal strategy is useless if an investor is forced to sell before its long-run mathematics has time to work.

In practice, many investors use fractional Kelly: half-Kelly, quarter-Kelly or another reduced fraction of the model's full recommendation. This sacrifices some theoretical growth in exchange for a smaller exposure to estimation errors and a less punishing path. It is not a universal magic number; the appropriate haircut depends on the quality of the evidence and the investor's actual constraints.

A useful discipline is to calculate the full-Kelly result as a reference point, then deliberately challenge it. What if the edge is half as large? What if losses are correlated? What if the trade cannot be exited at the price in the model? The answer after those questions may be a much smaller position—or no position at all.

06 / The caveat

The formula is only as honest as its assumptions.

Probabilities are not known

In markets, expected returns and probabilities must be estimated. If the edge is overstated, Kelly can recommend an exposure that is far too large. Parameter error is one of its biggest practical dangers.

Returns are not neat coin flips

Real assets have fat tails, jumps, changing regimes, gaps, illiquidity and trading costs. A model that assumes stable distributions can understate the possibility of extreme losses.

Investments interact

Portfolio positions are often correlated, and correlations can change under stress. A collection of individually sensible bets can create one concentrated portfolio risk.

Growth is not every investor's goal

Someone funding a near-term liability, protecting capital or facing a strict drawdown limit may prefer a lower-risk strategy. Maximising long-run log growth does not maximise every person's utility.

Full Kelly can be psychologically brutal even when the model is right. Long losing streaks and deep drawdowns are part of the distribution, not proof that the mathematics has failed. But in the real world, the investor's ability to stay invested is itself a constraint. A position size that causes panic, forced selling or a breached mandate is not practically optimal.

Fractional Kelly can soften the problem, but it cannot repair a false edge, missing tail risk or a broken portfolio model. The right response to unreliable inputs is not to apply a clever fraction mechanically. It is to improve the evidence, reduce exposure, impose risk limits or walk away.

07 / The practical takeaway

Protect the capital that lets you take the next opportunity.

Start with the edge, not the excitement. Estimate the probability and payoff as carefully as the evidence allows. Work out what a growth-optimal position would look like, then account for uncertainty, correlation, costs, liquidity and the investor's real-world tolerance for drawdowns.

In many situations, the sensible answer will be smaller than the mathematical maximum. That is not a failure of ambition. It is a recognition that compounding needs time, and time requires survival.

The Kelly Criterion's most useful lesson is not “bet bigger when you are confident.” It is more disciplined: even a genuine advantage can be destroyed by the wrong position size.
