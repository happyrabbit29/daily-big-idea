The Dutch
Disease.

How a commodity boom can make a country richer while quietly making its other exporters less competitive.

The idea in one sentence

A sudden export windfall can push up a country's real exchange rate and pull people and capital toward the booming sector, squeezing industries that sell into global markets.

REST OF THE ECONOMY
01 / The opening scene

The gas field that changed more than the energy balance.

In 1959, a huge natural-gas field was discovered near Groningen in the Netherlands. As production and exports expanded during the 1960s, the country gained a valuable new source of income. Gas brought foreign currency, public revenue and a stronger sense of national wealth. But an export boom can change more than the fortunes of the companies digging the resource out of the ground.

Imagine a Dutch factory making machinery for customers abroad. Its buyers compare it with machines from Germany, Japan and the United States. The selling price is largely set by international competition. Now suppose the gas boom pushes up the value of the guilder and raises wages and domestic service costs. The factory's product becomes more expensive relative to foreign alternatives, even though its engineers have not suddenly become less capable.

Some firms can absorb the pressure through productivity, better products or lower margins. Others lose orders. At the same time, mining, construction, government projects and local services may be competing for the same workers, engineers, land and capital. The country is richer, but parts of its older export base are under pressure.

A country can become richer from one export and less competitive in several others at the same time.

In 1977, The Economist popularised the phrase “Dutch disease” for the concern that followed the Netherlands' gas boom. The name stuck because it captures a counterintuitive possibility: the success of one sector can make life harder for the rest of the economy. It is a useful framework, not a claim that one gas field single-handedly caused every later problem in Dutch manufacturing.

02 / The mechanism

Two forces move beneath the headline numbers.

Economists often explain the effect using three broad parts of an economy: a booming export sector, other tradable industries that compete internationally, and non-tradable activities such as construction, retail and many local services. A resource windfall can affect the last two through two connected channels.

The Dutch disease mechanismFour steps · One economy-wide adjustment
01 / WINDFALLExport income risesOil, gas or minerals bring in foreign currency and higher profits or tax receipts.
02 / SPENDINGLocal demand expandsMore income is spent on local services, property, construction and public programmes.
03 / REAL APPRECIATIONDomestic costs riseThe currency strengthens, or local wages and prices rise relative to trading partners.
04 / CROWDINGOther exporters feel itTradable industries face higher relative costs and compete for labour and capital.

The exchange-rate channel and domestic-cost channel both matter. Under a fixed nominal exchange rate, local inflation can still produce a real appreciation even without a stronger quoted currency.

The spending effect

When a country earns more from a booming export, households, companies and the government may spend more at home. Demand rises for goods that cannot easily be imported, such as local construction, housing, personal services and some infrastructure. Their prices and wages can rise. Even if the nominal exchange rate does not move, domestic costs become more expensive relative to costs abroad. Economists call this a rise in the real exchange rate.

The resource-movement effect

The booming sector also attracts workers, contractors, engineers, investment and financing. Local suppliers expand to serve it. Other industries must pay more to retain staff and secure inputs, or they lose people and capital to the new opportunity. Resource extraction can be capital-intensive and employ relatively few workers directly, so this channel varies by industry and country. The spending effect can be more important than the direct movement of labour into the resource sector.

A miniature economy

Before the boom

Export price: $100

A manufacturer earns $100 from a foreign customer. Its local wages, rent and other costs leave enough margin to invest and compete.

After the boom

Local costs: +15%

Suppose wages and local inputs rise 15%, while the foreign selling price is unchanged. Unless productivity, product quality or the exchange rate offsets the increase, the manufacturer's margin is squeezed.

Illustrative arithmetic, not an estimate of any country's actual cost increase. The point is that a company can lose competitiveness without making a bad operating decision.

Put simply, the boom raises the price of local resources and services. A factory selling into a global market cannot always pass those costs on. A local hotel or construction firm often can. The relative winners can therefore shift toward non-tradables and the booming sector, while some exporters and import-competing manufacturers are squeezed.

03 / What it looks like in practice

Australia's mining boom: richer, stronger, and more exposed.

Australia offers a useful, more recent illustration. Strong demand for minerals, especially as China industrialised, helped drive a major mining investment and export boom from the early 2000s. The Reserve Bank of Australia noted that the world price of Australia's mining exports had more than tripled over the decade to 2014, while mining investment had risen from around 2% of GDP to 8%.

The boom delivered real benefits. In a 2014 research paper, the RBA estimated that by 2013 it had raised real per-capita household disposable income by 13%, lifted real wages by 6% and lowered the unemployment rate by about 1.25 percentage points. But the Australian dollar's appreciation weighed on other trade-exposed industries, including manufacturing and agriculture.

A measured trade-off · Australia, 2000s to 2013

The upside

+13% real disposable income

The RBA's model estimated higher household income, stronger real wages and lower unemployment as a result of the mining boom.

The pressure

−5% manufacturing output

The RBA estimated manufacturing output in 2013 was about 5% below what it would have been without the boom.

These are model-based estimates from the RBA's 2014 study, not simple before-and-after measurements. The study also found that the so-called deindustrialisation was not strong, partly because manufacturing supplied inputs to mining.

That last qualification matters. A resource boom does not automatically destroy every other industry. Australian manufacturers selling equipment and services to miners could benefit. The pressure was greater for firms exposed to international competition but less connected to the resource expansion. And the RBA's counterfactual estimate reminds us that the economy might have performed differently even without the boom.

The pattern is therefore not “mining good, manufacturing bad.” It is that one powerful source of demand changes relative prices and incentives across the economy. Some companies gain new customers; others face a higher cost base or a stronger currency. The same national shock can appear as an earnings tailwind in one business and a margin headwind in another.

04 / The policy response

Norway tried to separate earning the money from spending it.

A country receiving a large commodity windfall has a choice. It can spend much of the new income immediately, pushing demand into the domestic economy, or it can save a substantial share abroad and phase spending into the economy more gradually. Saving is not a magic cure, but it can reduce the pressure created by a sudden surge in domestic spending.

Norway built an institutional response around its Government Pension Fund Global. The fund was established in 1990, and petroleum revenues have been transferred into it since 1996. The fund invests abroad, rather than directing the entire windfall immediately into domestic assets and consumption. Since 2001, Norway's fiscal framework has guided the use of petroleum wealth by linking withdrawals from the fund to its expected long-run real return, while allowing fiscal policy to smooth economic fluctuations. The expected real-return estimate was reduced from 4% to 3% in 2017.

Norway's broad approachSave first · Phase in over time
01 / COLLECTCapture the rentState petroleum cash flows are transferred to the sovereign wealth fund.
02 / INVESTBuild foreign assetsThe fund invests internationally, spreading wealth across global markets.
03 / BUDGETUse a measured flowWithdrawals are guided over time by the fund's expected real return.
04 / SMOOTHReduce the spending shockGradual phasing helps avoid turning volatile petroleum income into volatile domestic demand.

This is a simplified description of the fiscal framework, not a guarantee that Norway is immune to exchange-rate, labour-market or political pressures.

The logic is intergenerational as well as macroeconomic: oil and gas are finite assets, so saving part of the proceeds converts underground wealth into a diversified financial portfolio that can support future budgets. Investing abroad also means that the initial windfall does not have to be spent entirely on domestic construction, wages and services.

There are trade-offs. A fund cannot prevent every relative-price adjustment. Domestic spending still occurs, and a strong export sector can remain a major influence on wages and investment. Nor is every country able to build the same institutions or sustain the same political discipline. The lesson is not “copy Norway's fund.” It is that the timing and location of spending can matter as much as the size of the windfall.

05 / Why investors should care

Do not stop your analysis at the commodity producer.

For investors, the obvious beneficiary of a commodity boom is the company selling the commodity. The less obvious question is what the boom does to the rest of the corporate landscape. A strong resource cycle can change foreign exchange rates, wages, power prices, construction costs, government spending, bank credit and the relative attractiveness of domestic versus export-oriented businesses.

A local engineering company serving mines may gain volumes. A manufacturer competing with imports may lose pricing power as its cost base rises. A retailer may benefit from higher household income. A utility may face higher demand and wage inflation, but also a larger industrial load. A bank may enjoy stronger loan growth while becoming more exposed to property prices and a concentrated regional economy.

An investor's checklist
  1. How much of the windfall is spent at home?
    Separate export receipts from domestic fiscal and private spending. The speed of spending can determine how intense the pressure on local prices and wages becomes.
  2. What is happening to the real exchange rate?
    Check the currency, domestic inflation, wages and unit labour costs. A stable nominal currency does not rule out a loss of competitiveness.
  3. Which firms are linked to the boom?
    Map direct suppliers, construction, logistics, engineering and services. Then identify exporters and import-competing businesses facing higher costs without a matching demand benefit.
  4. Can the gains survive a commodity reversal?
    Stress-test the currency, local wages, tax receipts, government capex and consumer demand if the commodity price falls. Costs and public commitments may not adjust as quickly as revenue.
  5. Is the economy building capabilities or just spending the rent?
    Look for investment in skills, infrastructure, productivity and diversified exports. A short-lived boom is less risky when it leaves behind productive assets rather than only higher recurring costs.

At company level, it is worth separating the effect on revenue from the effect on margins. A mining contractor may report excellent growth while wages, equipment costs and customer concentration rise at the same time. A non-resource exporter may report weak margins despite improving its own operations because the country's cost base has moved against it. Neither story is visible in a simple GDP growth number.

06 / The caveats

Is it really a disease if the country is better off?

Adaptation can be healthy

If a country has permanently become richer because it owns a valuable resource, workers and capital moving toward higher-value uses may be an efficient adjustment. A smaller manufacturing share is not automatically a national loss.

Transitions can be costly

Workers may not move easily between industries or regions. Firms can close, supplier networks can disappear, and industry-specific skills may take years to rebuild.

Other forces are at work

Manufacturing can decline because of technology, productivity, trade, demographics or changing consumer demand. A resource boom may coincide with those forces rather than explain all of them.

Every economy is different

Exchange-rate regimes, labour mobility, local supply chains, fiscal institutions and the size of the resource sector all affect the outcome. There is no single inevitable path.

The International Monetary Fund makes an important distinction: if a windfall is expected to last, a shift toward services and non-tradables can be a reasonable response to higher national income. The concern is greater when the boom is temporary, the economy makes irreversible adjustments, or the loss of capabilities damages long-run productivity. A country should not preserve every old industry at any cost. It should understand which capabilities are worth retaining and how workers and capital can move without being stranded.

That is why the phrase “Dutch disease” can be misleading when used as a verdict. It names a possible transmission mechanism, not proof that every commodity boom is harmful or that every manufacturing decline was caused by the currency. The real task is to measure the costs, benefits and transition risks separately.

07 / The practical takeaway

Follow the windfall through the whole economy.

When a country discovers a resource or receives a sustained commodity-price windfall, do not stop at export growth, royalties or the earnings of the mining companies. Trace what happens next: how much income is spent at home, whether the real exchange rate appreciates, where workers and capital move, and which industries can pass higher costs on to customers.

For an investor, the best question is not simply “Who sells the commodity?” It is “Who gains purchasing power, who inherits a higher cost base, and what happens to those businesses when the boom cools?” A resource windfall can create real national wealth. Whether it becomes broad, durable prosperity depends partly on how the country manages the adjustment.
