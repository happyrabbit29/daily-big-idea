The Option
to Wait.

Why an irreversible investment can be worth more when management keeps the right to delay, learn, stage the spending or walk away.

FIELD
NOTES
NO. 027
Edition 02725 November 2026Reading time · 8 minutes
The idea in one sentence

When a decision is difficult to reverse and new information is on the way, flexibility has value: a company may be better off preserving the right to invest than exercising it immediately.

A PROJECT IS
NOT ALWAYS A
NOW-OR-NEVER CHOICE
01 / The opening scene

The project has a positive NPV. Why not build it?

Picture an energy company reviewing a large offshore development. The engineering work is complete. The spreadsheet shows a positive net present value at the current oil-price assumption. The project team wants approval, suppliers are ready to negotiate, and the board has a familiar choice in front of it: invest or reject.

But one more seismic survey is due in six months. A new set of wells may clarify the size of the reservoir. The oil price is volatile. Steel and vessel costs could move. A production licence has a deadline, and a rival may be considering a nearby project. Once the company orders specialised equipment and begins construction, much of the money is sunk. It cannot simply sell half-built infrastructure at the price it paid.

The usual discounted-cash-flow calculation can describe the project under a set of assumptions. It is less comfortable with a third choice: spend a smaller amount now to keep the opportunity alive, learn more, and make the large commitment only when the picture is clearer.

Sometimes the most valuable thing a company owns is not the project. It is the choice to decide later.

This is the intuition behind real options. The word “option” is borrowed from financial markets, but the asset is real: a factory, mine, power plant, offshore lease, drug candidate, technology platform or expansion opportunity. The company has a right, not an obligation, to make a future investment after more information arrives.

02 / The mechanism

Three ingredients create the value of waiting.

Waiting is not automatically wise. A company should care about the option to wait when three conditions come together: there is genuine uncertainty, the commitment is difficult to reverse, and time is likely to produce useful information.

The real-options mechanismThree ingredients · Four choices
01 / UNCERTAINTYThe future is unclearPrices, demand, permits, technology or geological information may change.
02 / IRREVERSIBILITYCapital gets locked inSpecialised assets and sunk costs cannot be recovered at full value.
03 / LEARNINGTime reveals evidenceTests, customer orders, rival moves or market prices can improve the decision.
04 / FLEXIBILITYChoose after learningInvest, expand, stage, switch or abandon when the information arrives.

The option is valuable only if the company can preserve the opportunity and the information gained can change what it chooses to do.

Why an irreversible decision is different

Suppose a business can spend $100 million on equipment that will be worth much less if its product market disappears. If the market disappoints, the company cannot rewind the project and recover the full $100 million. That lost flexibility is a cost of committing now, even if a conventional project model does not display it as a separate line item.

By contrast, a company that waits may retain the right to invest later. If demand is strong, it can proceed. If demand is weak, it can walk away and keep most of its capital. It has not eliminated uncertainty. It has changed who bears the consequences of uncertainty and when.

Why information can be worth paying for

A pilot plant, a test well, a small product launch or an early clinical trial can be treated as a purchase of information. The first spend is not merely a miniature version of the final investment. It can reveal whether the larger investment deserves to happen at all.

This is why staged investment can be more valuable than a single large cheque. Each stage creates a decision point. Management can continue when the evidence is encouraging, change direction when the evidence is mixed, or stop when the economics no longer work.

03 / A small numerical experiment

Why the right to walk away changes the arithmetic.

Consider a simplified project that costs $100 million. Its value, once uncertainty clears, will be either $150 million in a strong market or $70 million in a weak market. Assume both outcomes are equally likely. To keep the arithmetic visible, ignore discounting, interim cash flows and the cost of waiting for a moment.

Illustrative project · Same assumptions, different choices

Commit immediately

Expected value: $110m

The average project value is (150 + 70) ÷ 2 = $110 million. Spend $100 million now and the expected surplus is $10 million. The company bears the weak-market loss as well as receiving the strong-market gain.

Wait, then choose

Expected surplus: $25m

After the market signal arrives, invest in the strong case and earn $50 million of surplus. In the weak case, do not invest and lose nothing on the large project. The probability-weighted surplus is (50 + 0) ÷ 2 = $25 million.

This is a deliberately stripped-down decision-tree illustration, not a valuation of a real asset. It ignores the discount for delay, lost production while waiting, holding costs, expiry and competitors. Those costs must be included before deciding whether waiting is actually better.

The difference is the asymmetry. Committing today exposes the company to both outcomes. Preserving the option allows it to participate in the upside while declining the investment in the downside state. The right to say “not yet” has economic value.

In a full model, the company would compare the present value of investing now with the value of waiting, including the expected information, the probability of each outcome, the cost of capital, the time remaining and the costs of delay. The option to wait is not a free bonus added to every project. It is a choice whose value depends on the facts.

04 / From theory to real assets

An offshore lease is not the same thing as a finished oil field.

Real-options analysis became especially useful in capital-intensive industries where the investment is large, the asset is specialised and the information arrives gradually. Petroleum exploration offers a natural example. A company may control a lease, collect geological data, drill exploratory wells and then decide whether the evidence justifies a much larger development.

In 1988, James Paddock, Daniel Siegel and James Smith published a study in The Quarterly Journal of Economics that applied option-valuation techniques to offshore petroleum leases. Their work treated a lease as a claim on a real asset and showed why conventional discounted-cash-flow methods could miss important timing choices. They also stressed that valuing the option requires a model of the underlying petroleum-reserve market; it is not enough to copy a formula designed for a traded share option.

A staged resource projectInformation before full commitment
01 / SECUREAcquire the opportunityObtain a lease, permit or exploration right with a finite life and real carrying costs.
02 / LEARNRun the first testsUse seismic work, samples or an exploratory well to reduce uncertainty about the resource.
03 / REASSESSUpdate the economicsRevisit recoverable volumes, prices, engineering costs and the development plan.
04 / EXERCISEDevelop or walk awayCommit the larger capital only if the updated opportunity clears the hurdle.

The stages are conceptual. Actual leases, permits, work commitments and development approvals have legal deadlines and obligations; they are not costless options.

Notice what the company is doing. It is not waiting passively for a better oil price. It is buying information, preserving a right to act and keeping the final commitment conditional on what it learns. An exploratory well can fail and still have been a rational information investment if it prevents a much larger loss on an uneconomic development.

The same logic appears in research and development. A pharmaceutical company does not have to fund every potential drug all the way from discovery to commercialisation in one decision. Laboratory work and clinical phases create successive gates. Evidence from an early phase can support further spending, trigger a redesign or end the programme before the largest costs arrive.

Research by Rita McGrath and Atul Nerkar examined patent-related R&D choices in pharmaceutical firms through the lens of real-options reasoning. Their study found patterns consistent with firms investing in opportunities that preserve future choices, rather than treating every research project as a single all-or-nothing commitment.

05 / The options inside a business

Waiting is only one kind of flexibility.

Real options are not limited to postponing a project. A company can design its investment so that it retains several different choices as the future unfolds.

Five forms of managerial optionality

Defer

Hold an investment right while waiting for a price signal, customer commitment or regulatory decision. The option expires or becomes more expensive to maintain.

Stage

Break a large commitment into smaller steps. Each stage creates a fresh decision point, as in exploration, clinical trials or a pilot production line.

Expand

Build a first unit with the ability to add capacity if demand proves strong. The initial asset creates a growth opportunity.

Switch

Design equipment or contracts so output, fuel, suppliers or operating modes can change when relative prices move.

Abandon

Retain the ability to stop or sell an operation when its economics deteriorate. Salvage value and exit costs determine how useful the option is.

Combine

Use partnerships, modular technology or joint ventures to share the early cost of learning while retaining a path to greater ownership later.

For an investor, these choices can matter as much as today's operating margin. A developer with a permitted site, grid access and a modular project may have more ways to respond to changing economics than a competitor committed to one huge design. A technology business with reusable intellectual property may be able to enter a second market without rebuilding everything from scratch.

But flexibility has to be real. A slide in an investor presentation saying “future expansion potential” is not an option if the company lacks land, permits, funding, technical capability or customer access. Optionality needs an asset, a right, a practical path to exercise and a plausible source of future information.

06 / Why investors should care

DCF asks what the project is worth. Real options asks what management can still choose.

A conventional DCF is useful because it forces the analyst to connect volumes, prices, operating costs, capital expenditure and cash flows. But a single forecast can make a flexible project look like a rigid one. It may assume that all capital is committed today, that the operating plan never changes and that management cannot respond when new evidence appears.

Real-options thinking adds a second layer. It asks whether the company can preserve an opportunity at modest cost, invest only after a meaningful signal, expand when demand is proven, switch when relative economics change or abandon before further losses accumulate. That flexibility can create value even when the first-stage project's standalone cash flows look modest.

An investor's checklist
  1. What is genuinely irreversible?
    Identify sunk costs, specialised equipment, non-refundable deposits, site development and assets with limited resale value. The harder it is to reverse the decision, the more important timing becomes.
  2. What information will arrive, and when?
    Look for a well result, customer order, permit, product test, technology milestone or market signal that can actually change the decision. Uncertainty alone does not make waiting valuable.
  3. What does it cost to keep the option alive?
    Include lease payments, maintenance, staff, financing, permitting, inflation and the risk that the opportunity expires. A right that is expensive to preserve may not be worth much.
  4. Can the company stage the commitment?
    Look for pilot projects, modular capacity, joint ventures, phased capex, contracts with break clauses or a sequence of technical gates. Flexibility should be visible in the operating plan.
  5. What would make management act?
    Ask for explicit triggers: a minimum price, utilisation rate, customer commitment, recovery factor or project return. Without decision rules, “we are preserving optionality” can become a polite description of indecision.

Capital-intensive companies deserve particular scrutiny. A utility planning a new generation asset, a miner considering a new pit, an oil producer evaluating a field or a manufacturer expanding a plant can all face the same question: should the company commit the full amount today, or is there a lower-cost step that produces information and preserves the choice to scale?

The answer can affect not only project NPV but also balance-sheet risk. If a business can delay, stage or abandon intelligently, it may avoid committing debt and equity to a poor outcome. But optionality can also create more risk when management uses flexibility to pursue additional uncertain projects. A right to expand is valuable only if the company can fund the exercise and earn an adequate return.

07 / The caveats

Waiting has a price. Sometimes the window closes.

Competition can punish delay

A rival may secure customers, a site, a licence, scarce equipment or a network position first. In a winner-takes-most market, waiting can hand away the opportunity.

Cash flows may be lost

A project that could earn revenue today may lose valuable operating years while management waits. The foregone cash flow is part of the cost of the option.

Rights can expire

Leases, permits, subsidies, purchase agreements and development windows can have deadlines. Carrying costs and legal obligations may rise while the company delays.

Models can look more precise than reality

Project volatility, exercise costs, probabilities and correlations are difficult to estimate. Real assets are not traded continuously, so financial-option formulas do not transfer perfectly.

There is another trap: uncertainty is not always reduced by waiting. A commodity price may remain volatile; political risk may grow; a new technology may make forecasts less rather than more reliable. Waiting only creates information value when the passage of time, a planned test or an observable event improves the decision.

Real-options analysis can also be abused. Managers may use “optionality” to defend pet projects, endless pilots or capital that never earns an adequate return. A genuine option should have a clearly stated opportunity, a cost of keeping it alive, a decision date or trigger, and a credible path to exercise or abandon it.

Academic reviews of real-options research have also noted that the empirical evidence and practical application are uneven. The framework is useful, but it does not remove judgement. In many corporate settings, a transparent scenario tree and explicit decision gates are more honest than a highly precise option value built on fragile assumptions.

08 / The practical takeaway

Do not confuse speed with decisiveness.

When a project is uncertain and difficult to reverse, the best decision may be to spend a little to learn before spending a lot to commit. But the company must compare the value of that information with the cost of delay, the risk of losing the opportunity and the cost of keeping the right alive.

For investors, look beyond the headline NPV and ask what choices remain after the first cheque is written. Can management stop, scale, switch or expand as evidence changes? Are those choices contractually and operationally real? Does the company have the balance sheet to exercise them?

The value of a project is not only what it can earn. It is also the value of the choices the company can still make along the way.
