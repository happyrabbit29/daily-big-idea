The Past
Has a Vote.

Why the first turn in the road can shape every turn after it, even when a better route appears.

Edition 0076 November 2026Reading time · 8 minutes
The idea in one sentence

Path dependence means that where a system ends up depends not only on its present conditions, but also on the sequence of choices and events that brought it there.

IS NOT JUST
BACKSTORY
01 / The opening scene

Imagine choosing a road before the map exists.

A city builds its first railway station on the eastern edge. Warehouses gather nearby. Workers move into surrounding neighbourhoods. Shops follow the workers; roads follow the shops. Decades later, the city’s business district, housing patterns and traffic flows all point east. A western site may now be cheaper and more convenient for a new station, but moving the centre of gravity is no longer a simple engineering decision.

The first station did not single-handedly determine the city’s future. It helped start a chain of mutually reinforcing decisions. Each later choice made the next one more likely. What began as one option among several became the route that everyone had to plan around.

That is the intuition behind path dependence: history can shape the menu of choices available today. The past does not merely explain how we arrived. Under certain conditions, it changes what is practical, profitable or politically possible next.

“The order in which things happen can matter as much as the things themselves.”
02 / Where the idea came from

From keyboards to economies.

Economists and historians became especially interested in path dependence when they tried to explain why one technology or institutional arrangement could dominate even when alternatives existed. In 1985, economist Paul A. David used the history of the QWERTY keyboard to illustrate how early adoption, training and coordination could reinforce a particular standard. His essay, Clio and the Economics of QWERTY, helped bring the idea into wider economic discussion.

W. Brian Arthur developed a more formal account of competing technologies in work culminating in a 1989 Economic Journal paper. His models showed how increasing returns to adoption can make a technology more attractive as more people use it. Small early advantages, including chance events, can then grow into durable dominance. The eventual winner need not be the one that would have won under a different sequence of events.

Political scientist Paul Pierson later applied the logic to politics and institutions. Policies create organisations, beneficiaries, expectations and routines. Those arrangements can generate support for their own continuation, making reversal difficult even when the original conditions have changed.

Path dependence is therefore not a universal law of history. It is an analytical framework: a way to identify when sequence, feedback and accumulated commitments make the past unusually influential.

03 / How the mechanism works

A small lead becomes a large head start.

Path dependence is easiest to understand when several forces reinforce one another. Economists often describe some of these as increasing returns: the benefit of choosing a particular option rises as that option becomes more widely used.

An early lead is not destiny, but it can compound

Conceptual illustration, not measured data. The routes represent possible histories, not a prediction that one must always win.

1. Learning effects

People and firms become better at using a technology through practice. Suppliers learn to produce for it. Costs fall, quality improves and expertise accumulates.

2. Coordination effects

A standard becomes more useful when others adopt it. A language, payment network or file format gains value because people can coordinate around it.

3. Complementary investment

Once a system spreads, other assets are built around it: training, infrastructure, software, supply chains and business processes.

4. Switching costs and interests

Changing course can require retraining, replacing equipment or upsetting groups that benefit from the existing arrangement. The cost is not always financial; it can be political or social.

04 / Work it through

Two apps, one early lead.

Imagine two fictional workplace platforms, Aster and Beacon. At launch, they are equally capable. A random large customer chooses Aster. That customer’s decision nudges a few suppliers to build Aster integrations. More customers then choose Aster because the integrations already exist. Consultants train in Aster, job applicants list it on their CVs, and companies write procedures around it.

A simple feedback loop

Assume each new customer values a platform for its basic features plus the number of useful partners already connected to it.

More users→More integrations→More usefulness→More users

By year three, Beacon might offer a cleaner interface or a lower subscription price. Yet a company considering the switch must also move its data, retrain staff, rebuild integrations and persuade partners to follow. Aster’s advantage is no longer just its original product. It is the ecosystem accumulated around it.

Notice what happened: no single customer needed to make an irrational decision. Each may have chosen the platform that looked safest at the time. Collectively, those sensible local choices reinforced one historical route.

This is the crucial distinction between being popular and being path dependent. Popularity alone is not enough. We need a mechanism that connects earlier adoption to later advantages, and evidence that a different sequence could plausibly have produced a different outcome.

05 / Where else it appears

One pattern, many arenas.

Technology and business: Operating systems, payment networks and industry software can become difficult to displace because users, developers, suppliers and training systems coordinate around a common standard. A challenger must often persuade an entire ecosystem, not just one buyer.

Politics and public policy: A policy can create beneficiaries, administrative agencies and expectations. Those groups may organise to defend it. Pierson’s work highlights why timing and sequence matter: a policy introduced during a brief political opening can shape institutions long after that opening closes.

Energy and infrastructure: A region built around coal mines, pipelines, refineries or a particular electricity grid accumulates specialised workers, equipment, transport links and local tax revenues. A cleaner technology may become cheaper, yet transition still involves retraining, replacement capital, grid upgrades and political bargaining. The old system’s footprint can slow the new one.

Everyday life: A first job can shape the skills you build, the people you know and the opportunities you hear about. A habit becomes easier through repetition; a friendship group influences what you do next. These are analogies to path dependence, not proof that an individual life follows the same formal dynamics as a technology market.

06 / The important caveats

History has influence. It does not have a remote control.

Path dependence is sometimes used too casually, as if every old institution or popular product must be trapped by its past. That makes the concept impossible to test. A useful explanation must identify the feedback mechanism, the sequence of events and the costs or barriers that sustain the outcome.

The famous QWERTY example also needs care. David’s essay made it an influential illustration of historical lock-in. But the claim that QWERTY is plainly and substantially inferior to alternatives, and survives only because history trapped users, has been challenged. In The Fable of the Keys (1990), economists S. J. Liebowitz and Stephen Margolis questioned key parts of the popular inefficiency story. The debate is a reminder: a compelling anecdote is not a settled empirical verdict.

Nor is persistence automatically inefficient. A shared standard can reduce confusion and coordination costs. A familiar institution may be imperfect but still cheaper than rebuilding everything. Sometimes the existing route survives because it remains genuinely useful, not because society is incapable of changing it.

Finally, lock-in is rarely absolute. New technology, regulation, crisis, changing prices or a coordinated coalition can create a break in the pattern. A path can be difficult to leave without being impossible to leave. The strongest version of the idea is conditional: when reinforcing feedback and switching barriers are strong, earlier events can have unusually durable effects.

07 / Try the idea on yourself

Three questions for the next decision.

  1. What is being reinforced?
    Pick a tool, habit or system you use. Is it still the best option, or is it simply surrounded by the most training, contacts, files and routines?
  2. What would have happened with a different first move?
    Imagine the same market, city or organisation starting with a different early choice. Which later decisions would change? This counterfactual helps separate history’s influence from today’s fundamentals.
  3. What would make switching worthwhile?
    List the real transition costs, including coordination and politics. Then ask what shock, incentive or shared standard could reduce them. If nothing could, you may be looking at a genuine lock-in. If several things could, the path may be sticky rather than fixed.

Follow the trail.

The reading below distinguishes foundational arguments from critiques. Journal pages may provide abstracts while full text can require institutional access.

  1. Paul A. David (1985), “Clio and the Economics of QWERTY,” American Economic Review, 75(2), 332–337. Foundational historical account.
  2. W. Brian Arthur (1989), “Competing Technologies, Increasing Returns, and Lock-In by Historical Events,” Economic Journal, 99(394), 116–131. Formal model of increasing returns and technological lock-in.
  3. Paul Pierson (2000), “Increasing Returns, Path Dependence, and the Study of Politics,” American Political Science Review, 94(2), 251–267. Application to political institutions and timing.
  4. S. J. Liebowitz and Stephen E. Margolis (1990), “The Fable of the Keys,” Journal of Law and Economics, 33(1), 1–25. A prominent critique of the standard QWERTY inefficiency narrative.
  5. W. Brian Arthur (1994), Increasing Returns and Path Dependence in the Economy. A collection of foundational work on positive feedback and lock-in.
