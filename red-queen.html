The race where
nobody wins.

A bacterium survives a drug. A new treatment arrives. The bacterium's descendants adapt again. Why staying alive can require running just to remain where you are.
Edition 004 · 3 November 2026 · Approx. 8-minute read
adapt / counter-adapt
Field note 004 / evolutionary arms race

A patient arrives at a hospital with a bacterial infection. Doctors choose an antibiotic that has treated similar infections for years. But the laboratory result comes back: the bacteria are resistant. The drug that once gave medicine a dependable advantage no longer works against this strain.

This is not a story about bacteria becoming clever or deciding to fight back. It is evolution doing what evolution does: variation appears, the environment filters it, and the survivors leave more descendants. The result can look like an opponent studying your strategy and inventing a counter-move.

The World Health Organization's global surveillance report, released on 13 October 2025, estimated that one in six laboratory-confirmed bacterial infections causing common infections in people worldwide in 2023 was resistant to antibiotic treatment. Between 2018 and 2023, resistance increased in more than 40% of the pathogen-antibiotic combinations monitored. The report drew on more than 23 million confirmed cases reported by 104 countries for 2023. These are surveillance estimates, not a census of every infection on Earth.

And the response is not standing still either. On 11 March 2026, WHO published new target product profiles to guide urgently needed antibiotics. It noted that 90 antibacterial agents were in preclinical or clinical development, but few candidates targeted priority bacterial pathogens and fewer still were considered innovative. On 25 May 2026, the World Health Assembly adopted an updated Global Action Plan on antimicrobial resistance for 2026–2036, built around coordinated action across human, animal, plant, food and environmental health.

Medicine is trying to keep its treatments effective while microbes continue to evolve. That is the doorway into the Red Queen hypothesis.

01 / THE ORIGINA line from a very strange chess game

The name comes from Lewis Carroll's 1871 book Through the Looking-Glass. Alice runs with the Red Queen, only to discover that the landscape around them keeps moving. The Queen explains that it takes all the running you can do just to stay in the same place.

“It takes all the running you can do, to keep in the same place.”
Lewis Carroll, Through the Looking-Glass (1871)

In 1973, evolutionary biologist Leigh Van Valen borrowed this image for a paper titled A New Evolutionary Law. He was trying to explain a fossil-record pattern he called the law of extinction: within a group, the probability of extinction appeared roughly independent of how long a lineage had already survived.

Van Valen proposed that species live among changing biological opponents and competitors. A lineage does not automatically become safer simply because it has existed for a long time. Other organisms are evolving too, changing the effective environment in which it must survive.

The idea later became closely associated with coevolution, especially the back-and-forth between hosts and parasites. The metaphor is now used more broadly, but its scientific meaning is narrower than “everything is always getting more competitive”.

02 / THE MECHANISMAdaptation meets counter-adaptation

Imagine a host species developing a defence that makes it harder for a parasite to infect. That defence gives the host an advantage. But parasites vary too. Individuals with traits that help them overcome the new defence leave more descendants. Over generations, the parasite population changes. The host is then under pressure to evolve again.

Neither side needs to plan. Natural selection is not a strategy meeting. It is a sorting process across generations: inherited differences affect survival and reproduction, so the frequency of those differences changes in a population.

The important point is that fitness is relative. A trait can be excellent against last generation's parasite and much less useful against this generation's. The environment is partly made up of other evolving organisms.

03 / A WORKED EXAMPLEHow a rare resistant bacterium takes over

Consider a deliberately simplified population of 1,000 bacteria. Suppose 990 are susceptible to a particular antibiotic and 10 carry a heritable resistance trait. Before treatment, resistant bacteria make up just 1% of the population.

Assume the treatment kills 99% of susceptible bacteria while resistant cells survive. About 10 susceptible cells and 10 resistant cells remain.

The resistant share has risen from 1% to roughly 50% among survivors. The antibiotic did not teach individual bacteria to resist it. It changed which inherited variants were more likely to survive and reproduce.

Real resistance is more complicated. It can arise through random mutations or by bacteria acquiring resistance genes from other bacteria. Different drugs, doses, tissues and bacterial species create different selection pressures. Resistance may also carry a biological cost when the drug is absent. The example isolates one mechanism so the arithmetic is visible.

This is an evolutionary arms race, but antibiotic resistance is not a perfect one-to-one match for every version of the Red Queen hypothesis. The drug is an environmental selection pressure; the bacteria evolve in response. In the wider public-health system, human use patterns, transmission, infection control and the development of new drugs all shape the contest.

04 / WHERE ELSE IT APPEARSMore than microbes and medicine

Immune systems

Hosts evolve ways to recognise pathogens. Pathogens evolve ways to evade recognition. Immune diversity can help populations avoid being vulnerable to one successful pathogen strategy.

Predators and prey

Better camouflage can favour sharper predator detection. Better detection can favour more effective camouflage. The cycle can produce escalating adaptations, though not every predator-prey relationship behaves this way.

Cybersecurity

Defenders patch vulnerabilities; attackers change tools and tactics. This resembles an arms race, but it is an analogy: human decisions, institutions and incentives are not genes and natural selection.

Business

A company improves its product; rivals copy or leapfrog it. The company must keep innovating merely to preserve its relative position. This is sometimes called a Red Queen effect in organisational research.

There is also a famous evolutionary connection to sexual reproduction. One hypothesis is that genetic mixing helps hosts produce rare combinations that parasites adapted to common host genotypes are less prepared to infect. This is one proposed explanation for why sex can persist despite its costs. It is not the only explanation, and its importance depends on the ecological and genetic details of the host-parasite system.

05 / THE REALITY CHECKIs evolution really an endless race?

Not always. The Red Queen is a hypothesis and conceptual framework, not a universal law requiring every organism to evolve continuously. Some interactions produce escalation. Others stabilise, weaken or settle into coexistence. Evolution can also be constrained by limited genetic variation, trade-offs, population size and the physical environment.

The distinction between the original macroevolutionary claim and modern host-parasite applications matters. A 2018 review by da Silva notes that the macroevolutionary version, used to explain constant extinction rates, is not strongly supported, while microevolutionary Red Queen dynamics in antagonistic coevolution, especially host-parasite systems, have stronger support.

Do not confuse the metaphor with a prediction. “An arms race” does not automatically mean both sides improve forever, that each response is equal, or that the eventual outcome is inevitable. Evolution has no finish line and no obligation to produce a winner.

The antibiotic-resistance example also shows why the human side matters. Evolution is biological, but the strength and direction of selection are influenced by choices about antimicrobial use, diagnostic access, sanitation, vaccination, infection prevention and the design of health systems. The WHO's 2026 action plan uses a One Health approach precisely because resistance crosses human, animal, plant, food and environmental systems.

06 / THINK ABOUT ITThree questions to carry with you

  1. Think of a skill you have improved. Has the standard around you also risen, so that the improvement mostly keeps you competitive rather than moving you ahead?
  2. In a host-parasite system, what might happen if one side evolves much faster than the other? Would that create a stable advantage, or a temporary one?
  3. In the antibiotic-resistance example, which interventions reduce selection pressure, which reduce transmission, and which create new treatment options? Why are all three needed?

The Red Queen changes the way we think about progress. Sometimes the opponent is not standing still. A better defence invites a better attack; a better attack invites another defence. What looks like a stable position from the outside can be the result of constant adaptation underneath.

The strange lesson is that survival may not look like getting further ahead. Sometimes it looks like running very hard simply to avoid falling behind.
